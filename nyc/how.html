How the Live Map Works

From data to a picture

The feeds above are only the foundation. Thousands of rows a day are no use to someone deciding whether to take the A train or worry about smoke on their block. The map's real job is to turn them into a few things you can take in at a glance, and to let you ask follow-up questions in place. Here is how it does that, and the exact rules it uses.

1. One pin says four things

Every incident is drawn the same way wherever it shows up (the map, the Now feed, the replay), so you learn to read it once. Try it:

🔥

Opacity = 0.45 + 0.55 × likelihood, so even an unlikely report stays visible but reads as tentative. The likelihood itself comes from the witnesses (the 🎯 box above).

2. What deserves your attention first

The Now tab doesn't list incidents by time; it ranks them by attention = (1 + severity) × likelihood × 1.5 if it started in the last hour. A confirmed serious fire from ten minutes ago outranks a dozen single-driver Waze reports. Alerts that have no place (citywide) go on top, and anything newer than your last look is marked new.

3. Less clutter as you zoom out

  • Groups. Below zoom 14, incidents, closures and news pins that sit close together become one count ("⚠ 15"), ringed in the worst severity inside, so one serious incident isn't hidden in a crowd of minor ones. Zoom in and they come apart.
  • Merging before drawing. Reports of one thing within 300 m become one incident; several Waze drivers reporting the same crash (same kind, within 250 m, within the hour) are one report. On 2026-10-29 that turned 2,285 "minor accident" reports in a day into 1,630.
  • Hotspots. The 👁 chip names the neighborhoods where crime and fire news and police or fire alerts gathered over 72 hours: each story counts once (however many outlets ran it), weighted 1 + its severity.
  • Presets. The Layers tab's Safety, Commute, Weather and Out tonight each switch on exactly the layers for that question, and everything else off.

4. Time

  • ⏱ Replay the last 24 or 72 hours. A moment shows what started in the 3 hours before it, older pins fading (opacity 1 − 0.65 × age / 3 h), so you see things appear and settle. Above the scrubber, a bar per half hour colored by its worst severity shows when the day was busy before you press ▶.
  • 🔎 This area draws the view's last 24 hours hour by hour, by kind.

5. Against what's normal

A count means little alone: 11 heat complaints is a lot for one ZIP in October and nothing in another. The 📈 unusual counts are shown as "× usual" against that ZIP's own expected rate (its last 28 days × the season), and only after a Poisson test and Benjamini–Hochberg (the 📈 box above). 🔎 This area shows 311's week against its usual the same way.

6. Questions about a place, answered in place

  • Click anywherethe address, the businesses there with ratings and opening hours, a street photo, directions, and a 🏠 building report (violations in plain words, complaints, bedbugs, housing court, the landlord's other buildings).
  • ▸ What's nearbyon every popup: today's incidents, the last 2 hours' alerts, closures and news within 500 m of that thing, with the nearest cameras.
  • 🔎 This areaeverything in the map's view: now, the last 24 hours, 311's week against usual, news, cameras, the official NYPD and FDNY record; ✨ asks the AI for a brief after it looks at the area's cameras.
  • 📍 Around mewhat's within 600 m of you: incidents and closures, subway stations with their alerts, buses, Citi Bike, cameras, restrooms.
  • ⭐ My placesyour saved spots (500 m) and routes (300 m either side): the chip counts what's new there since you last looked. Kept in your browser only.
  • 📸 and 📷street photos and live cameras to check with your own eyes, and the local vision model to read a camera for you.

7. Words, only from the facts

The 📝 City brief and the 📰 Research of one incident are written by models, but they are held to the data: the code numbers the facts, a bullet survives only if every fact it cites exists and every number in it appears in them, and each bullet links back to its facts (click one to pin them). Blip answers from the same store through its tools, and the places it names in an answer are pinned on the map for you.

What it can't see

  • No live 911 or police calls. NYC publishes 911 response times only as weekly citywide totals, and NYPD's call and complaint records with locations run months behind. The map shows them in 🔎 This area as the official record, not as what's happening now.
  • FDNY's dispatch file runs about three weeks behind. It's used to check, after the fact, which fire reports were real.
  • News is placed by a model. A story placed only at its neighborhood is drawn as a dashed circle, and a story alone counts as one witness.
  • Each source's reliability starts as a guess. It turns into a measured rate only as reports are confirmed or refuted; the table under "How likely is it real" shows how many each one has.
  • Unusual 311 counts are judged against the past. 311 runs about a day and a half behind, so "unusual" means yesterday's counts, not this hour's.

Everything runs on one Mac: the pollers, the database and the AI models. Keys for the feeds that need them stay on the server. Watched places live only in your browser.
