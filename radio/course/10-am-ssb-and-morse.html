AM, SSB and Morse

Before you startThis chapter uses:
  • Chapter 1: amplitude (the arrow's length)
  • Chapter 4: spectra and chapter 5: why a plain wave holds two mirror-image arrows
  • Chapter 9: the FM demodulator, for comparison

An arrow has two things you can change to carry a message: its speed and its length. FM (chapter 9) uses the speed. This chapter is about using the length, which is how radio started a century ago, and two clever descendants: single sideband, which keeps only the useful part, and Morse code, which just switches the arrow on and off.

You'll hear all of them on a real radio: aircraft talk to airports in AM (that's why pilots sound the way they do), radio amateurs talk across continents in single sideband, and Morse code is still alive on the ham bands.

AM: the sound is in the length

Amplitude modulation (AM) keeps the arrow's speed steady and makes its length follow the sound: longer when the speaker should push out, shorter when it should pull in. The demodulator is even simpler than FM's: measure each sample's length (chapter 2: the distance from the center), and remove the constant part.

Now tick Signal fades, and compare with chapter 9's FM demodulator (↩). There, fading changed nothing, because FM only looks at angles. Here the fade goes straight into the sound: the yellow line no longer matches the green one. When the signal weakens, the arrow gets shorter, and an AM receiver can't tell "shorter because the signal faded" from "shorter because the music went quiet". That's the familiar flutter of AM radio under a bridge or a power line, and the reason music moved to FM.

The readout points out AM's other weakness. The arrow always has some length, even in silence: that steady part is called the carrier, and it carries no sound at all, yet it takes most of the transmitter's power.

Sidebands, and why half of them are spare

Look at an AM station with chapter 4's winding machine. You see the carrier in the middle, and the sound twice: once above the carrier and once below it, as exact mirror images. These are the upper and lower sidebands.

Why two copies? It's chapter 5's fact again (↩): a plain up-and-down wave is two arrows spinning in opposite directions. AM changes the length with a plain wave (the sound), so every tone in the sound appears as an arrow above the carrier and its mirror twin below.

Since the two sidebands carry exactly the same information, a transmitter can send just one and drop the carrier entirely. That's single sideband (SSB). Even at full modulation, an AM signal spends two thirds of its power on the carrier and splits the rest between two identical sidebands, so only about a sixth of it is the sound you actually need. An SSB transmitter puts everything into that one sideband: on paper, it gets as far as an AM transmitter about six times as strong (with real speech the advantage is smaller, but still large), and it takes half the space on the dial. It's what radio amateurs and long-distance aviation use for voice.

Receiving SSB, and the Donald Duck problem

To play SSB, the receiver puts the sideband back where the sound belongs: from 0 Hz up to about 3 kHz. With the station's exact frequency at 0 (chapter 5) and the sideband kept by a filter (chapter 6), the sound is simply the I part (one shadow) of the arrows: the mirror twin we'd need for "both shadows" doesn't exist any more, so it doesn't matter that one shadow can't tell directions apart.

But there's no carrier to tell the receiver where "exactly 0" is. Tune slightly wrong, and every pitch moves by the same number of hertz:

Moving every pitch by the same number of hertz is not the same as moving them all up by the same musical interval. A voice's overtones sit at exact multiples of its base pitch (2×, 3×, …); shifted by a fixed amount, they stop being multiples, and the voice sounds like a cartoon duck. Ham radio operators tune SSB by ear until voices sound natural.

Morse code: on and off

The simplest message of all: switch the arrow on and off. Short on is a dot, long on is a dash. Radio people call it CW ("continuous wave", because the arrow itself never changes while it's on).

There's a catch: an arrow that's on doesn't make any sound by itself. FM listens to speed changes and AM to length changes; Morse has neither. The trick is to tune the receiver slightly off the station, so that its arrow lands a few hundred hertz from 0 and spins at that speed: an audible tone, called the beat. That's exactly the SSB receiver with a deliberate tuning error. Each dot and dash becomes a beep, and you choose the pitch by choosing the error.

Morse needs the narrowest space on the dial of any mode (well under 100 Hz) and can be read by a trained ear even when it's buried in noise. That's why it's still used today.

In code

AM (AmDemod in src/dsp.ts): the length of each sample, then a tiny filter that removes the steady carrier (it subtracts a slowly-following average; engineers call it a "DC blocker", because the steady part is the DC of chapter 4):

const x = Math.hypot(iq[2 * k], iq[2 * k + 1]);          // the arrow's length (chapter 2)
out[k] = this.y1 = x - this.x1 + 0.995 * this.y1;        // remove the constant part
this.x1 = x;

SSB (SsbDemod): shift the wanted sideband so it's centered on 0, filter away everything else (including the other sideband), shift it back, keep one shadow:

const shift = upper ? bw / 2 : -bw / 2;

this.up = new Mixer(fs, -shift);

Check yourself

Why does AM flutter under a bridge while FM stays steady?

AM reads the sound from the arrow's length, and a weaker signal makes the arrow shorter. FM reads only the angle, which doesn't change when the signal weakens.

Why can SSB drop one sideband without losing anything?

The two sidebands are mirror images of each other: they carry exactly the same information.

Why does a mistuned SSB voice sound like a duck, rather than just higher or lower?

Every pitch moves by the same number of hertz, so overtones stop being exact multiples of the base pitch. A real change of pitch would multiply all of them by the same factor instead.

Why is a Morse signal silent unless the receiver is tuned slightly off?

While it's on, the arrow is steady: no changing length or speed to turn into sound. Tuning off makes it spin at a few hundred hertz, which is audible.
