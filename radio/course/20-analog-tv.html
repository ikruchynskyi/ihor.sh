How analog TV worked

Before you startThis chapter uses:
  • Chapter 1: spinning arrows and chapter 2: arrows as numbers
  • Chapter 10: AM and sidebands
  • Chapter 17: a picture sent line by line, with sync pulses

Chapter 17's slow-scan TV takes two minutes per picture. Broadcast television sent thirty a second, in color, from the 1950s until 2009, when full-power US stations switched to digital (the last low-power analog ones went dark in July 2021). There's nothing left on the air to receive, so this chapter builds both ends: a transmitter that turns any picture into the exact signal a 1980s TV expected, and a receiver that decodes it again, live, in your browser.

Honest constraints first: an analog TV channel is 6 MHz wide, and the RTL-SDR captures at most about 3. You'll see below exactly what that costs.

The question to keep in mind: how do you fit a color picture into a signal that black-and-white TVs, built years earlier, still understand?

A spot that never stops moving

A picture tube has one electron beam making one bright spot. The spot sweeps left to right across a line, snaps back, drops a little, and sweeps the next line: a raster. Its brightness follows the signal's voltage moment by moment. Done fast enough, your eye blends the spot's trail into a whole picture.

NTSC draws 525 lines per frame, about 480 of them visible. Thirty frames a second would flicker, so each frame is sent as two fields: first the odd lines, then the even ones (tick "interlaced"). The screen is repainted 60 times a second for the bandwidth of 30: a clever trade from 1941.

525 lines per frame, 29.97 frames per second. How many lines does a TV draw each second?

\(525 \times 29.97 \approx 15\,734\). That's the whine an old TV made: a transformer driving the beam's sideways sweep at 15.7 kHz, which young ears can hear. (Black-and-white TV used exactly 30 frames and 15,750 lines; color slowed it by 0.1% for a reason in section 4.)

One line of composite video

The signal for one line is a voltage over 63.6 µs. It starts with a sync pulse, a dip below black ("blacker than black") that tells the receiver "start a new line now". Then a short pause with a burst of fast wiggles (the color burst, section 4), then 52.6 µs of picture: higher voltage, brighter spot. Levels are measured in IRE units: sync −40, blanking 0, black 7.5, white 100. Pick a row of the test card:

Why is the sync pulse below black, instead of being some special pattern inside the picture's range?

  • So it's louder than the picture
  • So nothing in any picture can ever look like a sync: a simple voltage threshold separates them
  • So the screen is darker
The same idea as chapter 17's 1200 Hz sync below 1500 Hz black. A 1950s TV separated sync with one transistor and a threshold; our decoder does the same thing in one line of code.

Where each field begins

Line syncs say "new line". To say "new field, back to the top", the signal dips to sync level for most of every half line, six times in a row: the vertical sync. Field 1's starts exactly on a line; field 2's starts half a line later. That half line is the whole trick of interlace: everything in field 2 lands half a line lower, between field 1's lines.

A real receiver doesn't trust each sync pulse on its own: noise can fake or hide one. It runs a flywheel: it expects the next sync one line later, and only nudges its timing a little toward each pulse it sees. Our decoder does the same, so snowy pictures still have straight edges (try the noise knob in the lab).

Color as a spinning arrow

Color had to be added in 1953 without breaking 20 million black-and-white sets. The solution: keep the brightness signal (Y) exactly as it was, and add the color as a faint, fast wiggle on top, at 3.579545 MHz, the color subcarrier. Old sets show the wiggle as fine dots too small to notice; color sets decode it.

The wiggle is chapter 1's spinning arrow. Its length is how strong the color is (saturation) and its angle is which color (hue), measured on two axes: blue minus brightness (B−Y) and red minus brightness (R−Y). Drag the arrow:

An angle needs a reference. That's the color burst: nine cycles of the subcarrier at the start of every line, always at the same angle (180°). The TV locks onto it and measures every color against it. (Phase errors on long cable runs turned faces green, so NTSC sets had a "tint" knob, and engineers joked the name stood for "Never Twice the Same Color".)

The subcarrier is 455/2 times the line rate: 227.5 cycles per line. Why half a cycle extra?

So the wiggle is upside down on every other line. On a black-and-white set, the dots from one line fall between the dots of the next, and the pattern nearly cancels to the eye. And it makes the next section's comb filter possible. Fitting 227.5 cycles exactly, together with the sound carrier at 4.5 MHz, is why color TV slowed the frame rate from 30 to 29.97.

Separating brightness and color: the comb

Brightness and color are added together in one signal. How does the receiver pull them apart? Because the color wiggle flips each line and the picture usually doesn't change much from one line to the next, two neighboring lines added together cancel the color and keep the brightness; subtracted, they cancel the brightness and keep the color. It's called a comb filter: its frequency response has teeth at exactly the subcarrier's harmonics.

The color that comes out is still riding on the subcarrier. To read its arrow, multiply by the subcarrier itself, sine and cosine (chapter 5's mixing, bringing 3.58 MHz down to 0 Hz), and average over one cycle (chapter 6). Sampling at exactly four times the subcarrier makes this neat: the sine is just 0, 1, 0, −1, repeating.

On the air, and what a dongle can catch

With a 2.4 MHz capture placed to keep as much picture as possible, what does the decoded picture look like?

  • Black and white and a bit soft: the color subcarrier (3.58 MHz above the picture carrier) is outside
  • Nothing at all
  • Full color but blurry
Brightness detail extends to 4.2 MHz, but most of a picture's energy is in the lower part, so 2 MHz still gives a recognizable picture, about as sharp as a VHS tape. The color killer sees no burst and turns color off, rather than showing random colors. Try it in the lab.

In code

The encoder and decoder are src/ntsc.ts, about 200 lines. The decoder's steps, in order:

// 1. sync: smooth, then a threshold halfway between sync (−40) and blanking (0)
if (sm[i] < -20 && sm[i - 1] >= -20) lowStart = fallAt(i);   // a falling edge, to a fraction of a sample
// 2. a dip longer than 18 µs is a vertical sync; half a line after the last line sync means field 2
const field = g - prevH < 0.75 * LINE ? 2 : 1;
// 3. the flywheel: expect each line one line later, nudge toward the edge actually seen
err = 0.7 * err + 0.3 * (measured - predicted);
// 4. the burst's angle is the reference for every color on the line
psi = Math.atan2(-qb, -pb);
// 5. comb: the sum of two lines is brightness, the difference is color
Y[j] = (x[k] + x[k - LINE]) / 2;  C[j] = (x[k] - x[k - LINE]) / 2;
// 6. demodulate: multiply by the subcarrier, average over a cycle, undo the burst's rotation
U[j] = p * Math.cos(psi) + q * Math.sin(psi);  V[j] = -p * Math.sin(psi) + q * Math.cos(psi);

Check yourself

What tells a TV to start a new line, and why can't the picture fake it?

The horizontal sync pulse, a dip to −40 IRE. Picture content never goes below black (7.5 IRE), so a threshold at −20 separates them.

How does a receiver know which field it's drawing?

Field 1's vertical sync starts exactly on a line boundary, field 2's half a line later. Measuring the distance from the last line sync tells them apart.

What do the length and the angle of the color arrow mean?

Length is saturation (how vivid), angle is hue (which color), measured against the color burst sent on every line.

Why does adding two neighboring lines remove the color?

A line holds 227.5 subcarrier cycles, so the color wiggle is inverted on the next line and cancels in the sum, while the brightness, nearly the same on both lines, doubles.
