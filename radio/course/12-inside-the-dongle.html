Inside the dongle

Before you startThis chapter connects the hardware to ideas you already know:
  • Chapter 5: mixing, and why one shadow makes two copies
  • Chapter 6: filters and chapter 7: decimation
  • Chapter 3: sampling and the 8-bit ruler

Since chapter 3 we've taken the I/Q numbers for granted: they arrive over USB, and we process them. This chapter opens the box. The surprise is how little in it is new. The dongle uses exactly the tools you've already learned (mixing, filtering, sampling, decimating), partly with electronics and partly with numbers, to deliver a 2.4 MHz slice of radio from anywhere between 24 and 1766 MHz.

Inside are two chips: a tuner (Rafael Micro R820T2), which handles the radio waves, and an RTL2832U, which turns them into numbers and talks USB. Between them they share one tiny component that sets the pace for everything: a 28.8 MHz crystal (a sliver of quartz that vibrates at one extremely steady rate, the chip's metronome).

Two words you'll meet from here on. Chips are controlled through registers: small numbered memory slots, each holding one byte, where writing a number changes a setting (one register picks the gain, three more set the tuning, and so on). And the two chips talk to each other over I2C ("I-squared-C"): a simple two-wire connection that lets one chip read and write another chip's registers. Our computer never talks to the tuner directly; it asks the RTL2832U to pass messages along (chapter 13).

The signal's journey

Predict: the dongle's converter is 8 bits at 28.8 MHz. Where does the tuner put a 98.7 MHz station before it's digitized?

  • It stays at 98.7 MHz
  • A few MHz up: an intermediate frequency
  • At exactly 0 Hz
A few MHz (the IF), slow enough for the 8-bit converter. Then the RTL2832U does chapter 5's mixing, chapter 6's filter and chapter 7's decimation in digital form.

Why an intermediate frequency, and the image problem

Why not just build a filter that picks out 98.7 MHz directly? Because a sharp filter whose center you can move anywhere from 24 to 1766 MHz is very hard to build. A sharp filter at one fixed frequency is easy. So radios since the 1920s have done it the other way round: move the signal to a fixed frequency, the intermediate frequency (IF), and filter there. This trick is called the superheterodyne, and it's in almost every radio ever made.

Moving the signal is done by mixing with a local oscillator running 3.57 MHz above the station. But there's a catch you already know: an analog mixer multiplies by a plain wave, one shadow, so everything lands in two places (↩ ch. 5). That means two different radio frequencies end up on the IF: the one you want, 3.57 MHz below the oscillator, and its mirror, 3.57 MHz above it. The mirror is called the image:

The defense is a tracking filter in front of the mixer (green): a broad filter that follows your tuning and weakens everything far away, including the image, 7.14 MHz above you. It's not very sharp (that's the whole problem), so a strong station sitting exactly on your image can still sneak through as a faint ghost. In the driver, choosing this filter's setting for each frequency band is the job of a lookup table (BANDS in src/r820t.ts).

The oscillator runs at 102.02 MHz so that 98.45 MHz lands at a 3.57 MHz IF. Which other frequency, in MHz, also lands at 3.57 MHz (the image)?

102.02 + 3.57 = 105.59 MHz: the same distance on the other side of the oscillator. With one shadow, the mixer can't tell them apart, so the tuner's filters must remove 105.59 MHz before mixing.

Making any frequency from one crystal: the PLL

The local oscillator has to run at exactly the right frequency: 102.02 MHz for 98.45, or 1093.32 MHz for planes at 1090. All from one 28.8 MHz crystal. The circuit that does it is a phase-locked loop (PLL): a fast, adjustable oscillator (the VCO) that's constantly compared with the crystal and nudged until its speed is an exact multiple of it. Think of a gear train: the crystal turns at one speed, and the gears set the ratio.

Type any frequency and see exactly how the tuner makes it. These are the same steps and numbers our driver uses:

  • The VCO can only run between 1.77 and 3.54 GHz, a range of exactly 2×. So any oscillator frequency can be reached by running the VCO at 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 or 64 times it, and then dividing down. That's why the tuner covers 24 MHz (1.77 GHz ÷ 64, minus the IF) to 1766 MHz (3.54 GHz ÷ 2, minus the IF).
  • The ratio to the crystal isn't a whole number, so the PLL uses a whole part plus a 16-bit fraction (a "fractional-N" PLL). The fraction's smallest step sets the tuning resolution: tens of hertz, far finer than any station's width.
  • After writing the registers, the driver waits 10 ms and checks a "locked" bit. If the loop hasn't settled, it boosts the VCO's current and checks again.

The VCO runs between 1.77 and 3.54 GHz and is divided by a power of two. Which divider gives a 102.02 MHz oscillator?

102.02 × 32 = 3264.6 MHz, inside the range (×16 = 1632 MHz is too low, ×64 = 6.5 GHz too high). Exactly one power of two always fits, because the range spans exactly 2×.
The math, step by step

\[ f_\text{IF} = f_\text{LO} - f_\text{RF}, \qquad f_\text{image} = f_\text{LO} + f_\text{IF} = f_\text{RF} + 2 f_\text{IF} \]

\[ f_\text{LO} = \frac{f_\text{VCO}}{2^k}, \quad 1.77\,\text{GHz} \le f_\text{VCO} \lt 3.54\,\text{GHz}, \qquad \frac{f_\text{VCO}}{2 f_\text{xtal}} = N + \frac{\text{frac}}{65\,536} \]

  • Example, 98.45 MHz: fLO = 98.45 + 3.57 = 102.02 MHz; image 105.59 MHz; k = 5 (÷32), so fVCO = 3264.64 MHz.
  • With the 28.8 MHz crystal, 3264.64 / 57.6 = 56.678: N = 56 and frac = 0.678 × 65 536 ≈ 44 419, the numbers in the trace above. The leftover rounding is why each tuning is a few hertz off.

In code

The PLL setup, from our tuner driver (setPll() in src/r820t.ts): find the divider, split the ratio into whole and fraction, write them, check for lock:

let mixDiv = 2, divNum = 0;
for (; mixDiv <= 64; mixDiv <<= 1) {                               // smallest power of two that puts the VCO in range
  if (freq * mixDiv >= vcoMin && freq * mixDiv < vcoMax) { for (let d = mixDiv; d > 2; d >>= 1) divNum++; break; }
}
const vcoFreq = freq * mixDiv;
const vcoDiv = Math.floor((this.xtal + 65536 * vcoFreq) / (2 * this.xtal));   // ratio × 65536, rounded
const nint = Math.floor(vcoDiv / 65536), sdm = vcoDiv % 65536;               // whole part, fraction
const ni = Math.floor((nint - 13) / 4), si = nint - 4 * ni - 13;
await this.writeReg(0x14, ni + (si << 6));
await this.writeReg(0x16, sdm >> 8);
await this.writeReg(0x15, sdm & 0xff);
// … wait 10 ms, read register 2, check bit 6 ("locked")

Check yourself

Why do radios move the signal to an intermediate frequency instead of filtering it where it is?

A sharp filter that can be moved across a huge range is very hard to build; a sharp filter at one fixed frequency is easy. So the signal is moved to the fixed frequency instead.

Listening at 100.0 MHz, where is the image?

The oscillator is at 103.57 MHz; its mirror on the other side is 103.57 + 3.57 = 107.14 MHz.

Why does the dongle's range stop at about 24 MHz at the bottom?

The VCO can't go below 1.77 GHz, and the biggest divider is 64: 1.77 GHz ÷ 64 = 27.7 MHz for the oscillator, which is 24.1 MHz minus the 3.57 MHz IF.

The analog mixer makes two copies. Why doesn't the digital mixer?

The digital mixer multiplies by a full spinning arrow (both shadows, I and Q), which moves everything in one direction only. The analog mixer can only multiply by one shadow (chapter 5).
