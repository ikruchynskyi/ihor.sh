Radio from scratch · Chapter 2

Arrows as numbers

In chapter 1, the game showed that one shadow isn't enough: to know which way an arrow spins, you need both its sideways shadow and its up-down shadow. So whenever a radio measures a signal, it writes down two numbers at a time.

This chapter is about what you can do with those pairs of numbers. Adding them turns out to be easy. Multiplying them turns out to be surprising, and that surprise is the engine of every software radio.

Here's a taste. Below is one arrow, called z. The dots show what you get by multiplying z by itself again and again: z, z×z, z×z×z, and so on. Drag the white ring to change z:

Multiplying makes the dots turn. Shorten z a little and they spiral inward; lengthen it and they spiral outward. Normal numbers don't do this: multiply 2 by itself and you just march off to the right, 2, 4, 8, 16. Why would multiplying arrows produce rotation? By the end of this chapter, it'll feel obvious.

An arrow is just two numbers

Put an arrow's tail at the center of a grid. To tell someone where its tip is, you need two numbers: how far it goes sideways and how far it goes up. Those are exactly chapter 1's two shadows: the green sideways shadow and the yellow up shadow.

Radio engineers call these two numbers I (sideways) and Q (up). When the radio dongle (the USB-stick radio receiver this course uses, an "RTL-SDR", about $30) sends data to your computer, that's literally what comes down the USB cable: I, Q, I, Q, I, Q, millions of times per second. Each pair is one arrow.

Notice there's a second way to describe the same arrow: by its length and its angle. Drag the arrow and watch both descriptions change together. They're two languages for one thing. Sideways-and-up is the language for adding; length-and-angle, as you're about to see, is the language for multiplying.

Adding arrows: tip to tail

You've already added arrows: in chapter 1, we attached a second arrow to the tip of the first. That's all adding is. Take arrow b, slide it (without turning it) so its tail sits on the tip of a, and the sum is the yellow arrow from the center to the new tip.

In numbers it's as simple as it looks: the sideways parts add up and the up parts add up, separately. Nothing surprising yet.

Multiplying arrows: turn and stretch

What should it mean to "multiply" two arrows? Let's think about what multiplying ordinary numbers does. Multiplying by 2 stretches: everything gets twice as big. Multiplying by −1 flips: 3 becomes −3, pointing the other way. A flip is a half turn.

So multiplication of ordinary numbers is already "stretch and turn", just with very limited turning: no turn, or a half turn. Arrows can point in any direction, so the natural extension is: multiplying by an arrow stretches by its length and turns by its angle. In other words:

  • the lengths multiply, and
  • the angles add.

Drag both arrows and check it:

The small arcs near the center show the angles stacking up: the product's angle is the blue angle plus the pink one.

Pause and ponderWhich arrow should you multiply by to turn something a quarter turn (90°) counter-clockwise, without stretching it? And what happens if you multiply by that arrow twice?

The answer to the first question is the arrow of length 1 pointing straight up. It's so useful it gets a one-letter name. Mathematicians call it i; radio engineers call it j (because "i" was already taken for electric current), and so will we.

Now the second question. Multiplying by j means "turn a quarter". Doing it twice means "turn a half", and turning a half means flipping: pointing the other way. So j × j = −1.

That's the famous "square root of minus one", and you've just seen there's nothing imaginary about it. In school it's often presented as a strange rule to memorize. It's simply what a quarter turn does when you do it twice.

A game: land on the target

The blue arrow is fixed. Find the pink multiplier that sends the yellow product into the white ring. Think in turns and stretches: how far round does blue need to go, and how much longer or shorter does it need to get?

After a few rounds you'll notice that you stop fiddling and start aiming: point the multiplier at the angle you need to turn by, and make it as long as the stretch you need. That instinct is the whole of complex multiplication.

Multiply again and again, and it spins

Now let's go back to the opening spiral and understand it. Start with the arrow pointing at 1. Multiply it by a small "step" arrow. Then multiply the result by the same step again. And again. Each multiplication turns by the step's angle and stretches by the step's length:

  • Stretch exactly ×1.00: the arrow goes round and round a circle forever. This is chapter 1's spinning arrow, now made from nothing but repeated multiplication.
  • A little less than 1: it spirals in and dies away. A little more: it spirals out and grows. Neither is what a radio wants, so the step's length must stay exactly 1.
  • Make the turn negative: it spins the other way, clockwise. That's a negative frequency, the thing one shadow couldn't detect in chapter 1's game.
  • Bigger turns per step mean faster spinning: the turn per step is the frequency, just counted in "degrees per snapshot" instead of "turns per second".

Look at the dots on the right too: the up part (Q) of each step, one after another. It's chapter 1's sine wave again, but drawn as separate dots instead of a smooth line, because a computer only ever sees the arrow at separate moments. Those moments are called samples, and chapter 3 is about them.

The arrow at angle θ, built from tiny turns

One last idea, and it explains a formula you may have seen and been confused by.

Here's a strange-sounding way to build a turn. Start at 1 and take a small step sideways: multiply by an arrow that's 1 to the right and a tiny bit up. Because that step is perpendicular to where you're pointing, it mostly turns you and barely stretches you. Do lots of those small steps in a row, and you curl around the circle.

Below, the total amount of "sideways" is θ (the Greek letter theta), shared out over n steps: each step multiplies by 1 + j·θ/n. The ring on the circle marks the arrow at angle θ, the place we'd like to land.

With one big step, you overshoot badly: one big sideways jump makes the arrow much longer than 1. Now raise n. The steps get smaller, each one stretches less, and the path hugs the circle more and more tightly, landing almost exactly on the ring: length 1, angle θ.

The limit of this process, infinitely many infinitely small sideways steps, has a famous name: ejθ. That's all ejθ means: the arrow of length 1 at angle θ, reached by turning smoothly. (The θ here is in radians: 6.28, i.e. 2π, is a full turn, as in chapter 1.)

Pause and ponderSet θ to 3.14 (that's π, half a turn) and n to 60. Where do you land? You've just seen why ejπ = −1, the equation people call the most beautiful in mathematics. It says: turning half way round the circle flips you to the opposite side.

Writing it down

Here is the notation, now that you know what each piece means. The colors match the scenes.

z=I+j·Q
  • An arrow is written as its sideways part plus j times its up part. The "j ·" just marks which number is the up one. This is called a complex number ("complex" in the sense of "made of two parts", not "complicated").
(a+jb)·(c+jd) = (ac−bd)+j(ad+bc)
  • This is "lengths multiply, angles add", written in the sideways-and-up language. You don't need to memorize it: multiply out the brackets like ordinary algebra, and wherever j·j appears, replace it with −1 (a quarter turn twice is a flip). That single substitution is where the −bd comes from.
ejθ=cos⁡θ+j·sin⁡θ
  • The arrow of length 1 at angle θ. Its sideways shadow is cos θ and its up shadow is sin θ, exactly as in chapter 1.
ej·2πft
  • Chapter 1's spinning arrow in one symbol: at time t, the angle is 2π·f·t, so the arrow turns f times per second. A negative f spins clockwise.

In code

From this chapter on, every idea ends as code: the real lines that run the receiver on this site.

The arrows arriving from the dongle. The USB cable delivers bytes from 0 to 255, alternating I, Q, I, Q. A byte of 127.5 means zero, so each one is shifted and scaled to a number between −1 and +1 (src/iq.ts):

for (let i = 0; i < out.length; i++) out[i] = (u[i] - 127.5) / 127.5;
// out = [I0, Q0, I1, Q1, I2, Q2, …]: one arrow per pair

Multiplying arrows. Here is the heart of the receiver's mixer (src/dsp.ts), which you'll meet properly in chapter 5. For every incoming arrow (iq[k], iq[k+1]) it multiplies by a spinning arrow (c, s), using exactly the formula above with a = I, b = Q, c = cos, d = sin:

const c = Math.cos(ph), s = Math.sin(ph);   // the spinning arrow, at angle ph
out[k]     = iq[k] * c - iq[k + 1] * s;     // sideways: ac − bd
out[k + 1] = iq[k] * s + iq[k + 1] * c;     // up:       ad + bc
ph += this.step;                            // turn a little further for the next sample

Notice what it doesn't do. The spinning-arrow scene made the arrow by multiplying by a step arrow over and over, which is elegant. But a computer rounds every multiplication very slightly, and those tiny errors keep adding up: the length slowly drifts away from exactly 1, turning the circle into a very slow version of the spirals in that scene. (Chapter 5 measures it: after 10 million steps with the 32-bit numbers signal code often uses, the length is off by about 0.0006%. Tiny, but it never stops growing.) So the real code keeps a running angle and computes each arrow fresh from it. Same idea, nothing to accumulate.

Check yourself

Multiply an arrow of length 2 at 10° by an arrow of length 3 at 80°. What do you get?

Length 2 × 3 = 6, angle 10° + 80° = 90°: an arrow of length 6 pointing straight up, which is 6j.

What does multiplying by −1 do to an arrow, in turning language?

Turns it half way round (180°) without stretching. −1 is the arrow of length 1 at 180°.

Why is j × j = −1, without using any rules?

Multiplying by j turns a quarter. Doing it twice turns a half. Turning a half points the other way, which is what multiplying by −1 does.

You multiply by a step arrow of length 0.99 over and over. What happens, and why can't a radio's spinning arrow do that?

It spirals inward, losing 1% of its length every step, and soon fades to nothing. A radio's reference arrow must keep exactly the same length forever, which is also why our code recomputes it from an angle instead of multiplying repeatedly.

An arrow turns −30° per sample. What's special about it compared with one turning +30°?

It spins clockwise: a negative frequency. With both I and Q you can tell the two apart; with only one shadow you couldn't (chapter 1's game).
