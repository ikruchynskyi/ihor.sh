Digital pictures from space

Before you startThis chapter uses:

Russia's Meteor-M weather satellites circle the Earth from pole to pole, 830 km up, and broadcast what their cameras see, unencrypted, to anyone with a $30 dongle and a homemade antenna. The signal is called LRPT (low rate picture transmission): around 137.1 or 137.9 MHz, 72,000 symbols a second, a strip of picture at a time as the satellite sweeps over you. Chapter 20's TV was analog: the voltage was the brightness. This one is digital: numbers, packed, protected and modulated. Everything that makes digital radio work is in it.

This chapter builds a link with the same structure (we simplified two layers, noted where they come up) and decodes a whole simulated pass in your browser. The question to keep in mind: how can a picture come through perfectly when a fifth of the bits arrive wrong?

A pass, and a frequency that slides

A satellite in low orbit is above your horizon for at most about 15 minutes, a few times a day. Coming toward you, its signal is squeezed to a higher frequency; going away, stretched lower: the Doppler shift, the same thing that drops an ambulance siren's pitch as it passes. At 137.9 MHz and 7.45 km/s it slides by about ±3 kHz. Choose how high the pass goes:

The Doppler shift changes fastest when the satellite is…

  • rising, just over the horizon
  • at its highest point, flying past
  • the shift is constant through the pass
Overhead, its motion swings from "toward you" to "away from you" in a few minutes, so the curve is steepest there (up to about 50 Hz a second on an overhead pass). The receiver has to track that, which is section 3's job.

Four arrows for two bits: QPSK

The carrier is chapter 1's spinning arrow again, and each symbol sets its angle to one of four positions, 45°, 135°, 225° or 315°: QPSK, two bits per symbol. Plotting each received symbol as a dot gives a constellation: four tight clusters when all is well. Noise blurs the clusters. A frequency error is worse: every symbol is rotated a little more than the last, and the four clusters smear into a ring.

Es/N0 is the energy in one symbol over the noise's energy per hertz: the SNR that matters for digital links. The left picture is what the receiver gets; the right one is after it measured and removed the frequency error, then kept tracking it.

Finding the frequency: the 4th-power trick and the Costas loop

To remove the Doppler, the receiver first has to measure it, without knowing the data. The trick: raise every symbol to the 4th power (as a complex number, chapter 2: multiply its angle by four). The four QPSK angles, 45° + 90°·k, all become 180°: the data disappears, and what's left spins at exactly four times the frequency error. One FFT (chapter 4) of those 4th powers shows a single spike; divide its frequency by four.

\[ z = e^{j(\pi/4 + k\pi/2 + \Delta\omega\, n)} \quad\Rightarrow\quad z^4 = e^{j(\pi + 2k\pi + 4\Delta\omega\, n)} = -e^{j\,4\Delta\omega\, n} \]

Then a Costas loop keeps it locked as the Doppler slides: for each symbol, compare it to the nearest of the four points, and nudge the receiver's phase (and, more slowly, its frequency) by the angle it's off. It's chapter 11's control loop, steering an angle instead of a clock.

A Costas loop can lock with the constellation rotated by 0°, 90°, 180° or 270°: all four look identical. How does the receiver find out which is right?

  • The loop always locks at 0°
  • From the carrier's phase
  • It decodes all four, and keeps the one where the known sync word shows up
Every frame starts with the same 32-bit sync word, 0x1ACFFC1D. Decoded with the wrong rotation, the bits are garbage and the word never appears; with the right one, it appears every frame. Our receiver runs four decoders until one finds two sync words, then keeps only that one.

Fixing errors before they happen: the convolutional code

At the edge of a pass, a few percent of the symbols are on the wrong side of a line. Instead of sending each bit once, the transmitter sends two bits for every bit of data, each a different mix (XOR) of the last seven data bits. Every data bit leaves traces in 14 transmitted bits, so a few of them can be wrong and the others still agree on what it was. The decoder, the Viterbi algorithm, keeps 64 guesses at once (one per possible state of those seven bits), scores each by how well it explains what arrived, and at every step throws away the worse of each pair. It's not magic: errors bunched too close together defeat it, and below about Es/N0 = 4 dB there are simply too many. Then the picture doesn't get worse gradually as an analog picture would; it goes from perfect to nothing within a decibel or two: the cliff effect of digital radio. (The real link adds a second layer, Reed-Solomon, which repairs most of what Viterbi misses. Ours uses a simpler checksum instead: a damaged frame is just dropped.)

The code is "rate ½": two coded bits per data bit. LRPT sends 72,000 QPSK symbols a second. How many thousand data bits per second is that?

72,000 symbols × 2 bits = 144,000 coded bits, half of them data: 72 kbit/s. Half the capacity spent on protection, and worth it: without the code the picture would be unusable for most of a pass.

Pictures in 8×8 blocks

72 kbit/s is slow for pictures, so they're compressed the JPEG way. Each 8×8 block of pixels is rewritten as 64 numbers saying how much of each of 64 standard patterns it contains, from "flat" through "gentle slope" to "fine checkerboard" (a 2-D version of chapter 4's Fourier transform, the DCT). Clouds and coastlines are mostly smooth, so the first few numbers carry almost everything and the rest can be dropped or rounded coarsely:

Meteor sends three such pictures at once, from different wavelengths (visible light and infrared: infrared sees clouds at night by their cold), which decoders combine into false-color images. Ours sends one.

In code

The link is src/lrpt.ts, about 300 lines, transmitter and receiver. The Viterbi decoder's heart, for each received pair of soft values a, b:

for (let ns = 0; ns < 64; ns++) {                       // each state: the last 6 data bits
  const u = ns >> 5, p0 = (ns & 31) << 1, p1 = p0 | 1;    // the two states it can come from
  const e0 = exp[(u << 6) | p0], e1 = exp[(u << 6) | p1];  // the bit pairs each path would have sent (±1)
  const m0 = m[p0] + a * e0[0] + b * e0[1], m1 = m[p1] + a * e1[0] + b * e1[1];
  if (m1 > m0) { n[ns] = m1; d[ns] = 1; } else n[ns] = m0;  // keep the better path, remember which
}

The receiver, symbol by symbol: matched filter → Gardner timing → AGC → (once) the 4th-power FFT → Costas loop → four rotations into four Viterbi decoders → sync word search → descramble → checksum → picture.

The first 4 frames of each run go to measuring the frequency and finding the rotation. At 4 dB nearly everything still comes through; at 2 dB the frequency is still found (998 Hz for 1000), but the code gives up: the cliff.

Lab: a pass, decoded live

A whole pass, sped up: the satellite rises, the signal strengthens as it comes closer, the Doppler slides through zero, and it sets. Every frame is a strip of picture, encoded, modulated, Doppler-shifted, buried in noise, and decoded by the receiver above, as it arrives.

For real passes: a V-dipole (two 53 cm rods at 120°, horizontal) outdoors, a dongle at 137.9 MHz recording 288 kS/s or more, and SatDump, which implements the full LRPT stack including the parts simplified here. Pass times for your location: n2yo.com (search "METEOR-M").

Check yourself

Why does the frequency of a satellite's signal slide during a pass?

Doppler: approaching, the waves are compressed (higher frequency); receding, stretched (lower). The change is fastest as it flies past overhead.

How does the 4th power remove QPSK's data?

Multiplying angles by four turns all four QPSK angles (45° + k·90°) into 180°, so only the frequency error remains, at four times its size.

What does a sync word do besides marking frames?

It resolves the 90° ambiguity of the Costas loop: only the right rotation decodes to the known pattern.

Why do digital pictures fail suddenly instead of getting gradually snowy?

The error-correcting code fixes everything up to a point, so the picture stays perfect; past it, errors overwhelm the code and whole frames are lost.
