From numbers to speakers

Before you startThis chapter uses:
  • Chapter 8: chunks and the audio cushion
  • Chapter 3: samples are snapshots at a fixed rate
  • Chapters 9 and 10: where the sound comes from

The demodulator hands us numbers: about 48 000 per second, each saying where the speaker cone should be. Getting them to come out of the speakers smoothly, for hours, turns out to involve four practical problems that every radio, phone and video call has to solve. None of them is hard, but each one, ignored, makes the radio sound broken.

Two clocks that never agree

The dongle decides how fast samples arrive, using its own crystal (28.8 MHz). The sound card decides how fast they're played, using a different crystal. No two crystals are exactly alike: they differ by some parts per million (ppm). 50 ppm sounds like nothing, but it means that every hour, one side gets 180 ms ahead of the other.

Chapter 8's audio cushion (↩) absorbs this, for a while. Here's the cushion over four hours:

If the dongle runs fast, sound piles up faster than it's played, until the queue is more than 600 ms long; then our player drops a chunk, a barely noticeable skip. If the dongle runs slow, the queue drains until it's nearly empty; then the player waits to rebuild the cushion, a short silence. Either way, one small glitch every hour or two. Professional systems avoid even those by measuring the drift and stretching or squeezing the sound by a tiny amount to match (another use of the rate conversion below).

Playing at a different rate

Our sound comes out at whatever rate the last decimation gave (chapter 7): for FM at 2.4 MS/s, exactly 50 000 samples per second. The sound card might run at 44 100 or 48 000. So someone has to produce new samples between the ones we have, at different moments: resampling.

The simplest way to guess a value between two samples is to draw a straight line between them and read off the height. It's not exact, since the real sound curves, but the closer together the samples are, the smaller the error: with 10 samples per wave it's under 5%, with 50 it's 0.2%. Our audio has well over 10 samples per wave for every note that matters, and browsers use smarter curves than straight lines. In this site's receiver, the browser's Web Audio system does the resampling: we tell it our rate, and it converts.

Automatic volume

FM's output level depends only on how loud the music is (chapter 9), so a fixed volume works. But AM and SSB get louder and softer with the signal strength (chapter 10), which changes with distance, weather and every passing bus. A receiver therefore adjusts its own volume: automatic gain control (AGC).

Ours follows the loudest recent peak and divides by it. When the sound gets louder, it reacts instantly (the "attack"), so a sudden loud signal never blasts your ears. When the sound gets quieter, it raises the volume slowly (the "release"). Make the release very short and listen to the result in the picture: the quiet passages get pumped up to full volume right away, so loud and quiet parts of speech end up equally loud, and the background noise "breathes" up in every pause. Make it very long and a fade stays quiet for seconds. Around half a second is a common compromise.

Squelch: silence between transmissions

Two-way radio (airport towers, police, taxis, hams) is silent most of the time. A receiver tuned to an empty channel plays pure noise: a loud hiss, for minutes, between every short message. A squelch mutes the sound whenever the channel is too weak to contain a transmission:

The squelch measures the channel's strength every 20 ms (the average length² of its arrows, in dB: chapter 4's power). Above the threshold, the sound is on; below it, muted. Two details make it pleasant to use:

  • Smoothing: the measurement is averaged over a few chunks, so a single noisy spike doesn't open it.
  • Hysteresis: it opens at the threshold, but only closes again 3 dB below it. Without that gap, a signal hovering right at the threshold would chop the sound on and off many times a second.

Set the threshold too low and noise gets through; too high and the start of weak messages is cut off. Real radios have a squelch knob for exactly this.

In code

Scheduling sound (LivePlayer in src/course/audio.ts): each chunk is scheduled to start exactly when the previous one ends, with the cushion and the two drift rules. (Spectrum Lab has since moved to one continuous stream that corrects drift smoothly instead: chapter 19.)

if (this.t < now + 0.05) this.t = now + 0.15;   // ran dry: rebuild a 150 ms cushion
if (this.t > now + 0.6) return;                 // too far ahead (clocks drift apart): drop this chunk
const buf = ctx.createBuffer(1, samples.length, rate);   // we say our rate; the browser resamples
src.start(this.t);  this.t += buf.duration;

Automatic volume (Agc in src/dsp.ts): instant attack, slow release:

const a = Math.abs(x[i]);
this.env = a > this.env ? a : Math.max(1e-4, this.env * this.decay);   // jump up, sink slowly
out[i] = (x[i] * target) / this.env;

Squelch (Squelch in src/dsp.ts): power in dB, smoothed, with 3 dB of hysteresis:

const db = 10 * Math.log10(p / (channel.length / 2) + 1e-20);   // average power of this chunk
this.level = 0.7 * this.level + 0.3 * db;                       // smoothing
if (!this.open && this.level > this.threshold) this.open = true;
else if (this.open && this.level < this.threshold - 3) this.open = false;

Check yourself

Two clocks differ by 100 ppm. How far apart do they get in an hour?

100 millionths of 3600 seconds: 0.36 seconds, i.e. 360 ms per hour.

Why does AGC react instantly to loud sounds but slowly to quiet ones?

Reacting slowly to a sudden loud signal would blast your ears for a moment. Reacting quickly to quiet moments would pump the volume up in every pause, making background noise surge.

Why does a squelch close a little below the level where it opens?

So a signal hovering right at the threshold doesn't make it flicker on and off. The 3 dB gap means a signal must clearly drop before the sound is cut.
