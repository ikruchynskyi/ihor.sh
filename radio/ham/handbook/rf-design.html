RF design labs

Six ideas that radio designers use every day, each as something you can push around. They go a step beyond the license lessons, filling gaps for readers working through a radio engineering text like the ARRL Handbook. Each lab says which earlier lessons it builds on.

Getting the heat out

Every watt a transistor wastes becomes heat at its junction, and the heat has to flow out through a chain of materials, each with a thermal resistance in °C per watt: junction to case, case to heat sink (through the insulating pad and grease), heat sink to air. Like resistors in series (↩ lesson 1), temperature rises add up:

The junction temperature is the air temperature plus power × (sum of thermal resistances). Silicon is usually rated to 150–175 °C at the junction, and lifetime falls fast as you approach it. That's why a linear amplifier, which wastes about half its input (↩ amplifier classes), needs a big heat sink and a fan.

Designing a matching network

Two reactive parts, a series one and a shunt one, can transform any load to 50 Ω. On a Smith chart (↩ lesson 8), adding a series reactance moves the point along a circle of constant resistance; adding a shunt one moves it along a circle of constant conductance. The design is a path from the load to the center:

There are always two solutions: one tends to be a low-pass network (series inductor, shunt capacitor), handy for cleaning up harmonics; the other a high-pass. The bigger the ratio between the two resistances, the higher the network's Q, and the narrower the band it matches over (↩ lesson 4). Transmitter output networks (the Pi and Pi-L of lesson 4) are this idea with a third part to choose the Q.

Noise figure: the first stage decides

Every stage of a receiver adds noise. But noise added after a stage with gain counts for less, because the signal has already been amplified. The total noise figure follows the Friis formula: the first stage's noise figure, plus each later stage's excess noise divided by all the gain before it. Move the preamplifier around:

A lossy cable in front of the receiver adds its whole loss to the noise figure (a passive loss of L dB has a noise figure of L dB). A low-noise preamp before the loss hides it. That's why VHF/UHF weak-signal stations put the preamp at the top of the mast. On low HF, as lesson 11 showed, the sky noise is so strong that none of this matters (↩).

The simulated filter and the real one

Circuit simulators are wonderful, but they only know what you tell them. Here's a 5-element low-pass filter for 40 meters (a classic transmitter harmonic filter), simulated with ideal parts, and then with the small parasitics every real part has: inductance in capacitor leads, capacitance between coil turns, resistance in the wire (↩ lesson 4: parts aren't ideal):

The ideal filter keeps rejecting forever. The real one comes back up at VHF: each capacitor with its lead inductance becomes a series-resonant circuit, and each coil with its turn-to-turn capacitance becomes parallel-resonant, so above those frequencies the parts stop behaving like what's printed on them. Real designs keep leads short, use chip parts, and are checked on a network analyzer; at microwaves, designers move to electromagnetic simulation of the actual copper.

Phase noise and reciprocal mixing

No oscillator is perfectly pure: its energy is spread into skirts on either side, its phase noise, measured in dB below the carrier per hertz (dBc/Hz) at some offset. Straightening an amplifier: predistortion

A power amplifier near its limit compresses its peaks, which creates intermodulation splatter (↩ lesson 10). Modern SDR transmitters measure the amplifier's curve and bend the signal the opposite way before it gets there, so the two distortions cancel.
