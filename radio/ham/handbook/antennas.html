Antennas in practice

Before you startThis lab builds on:
  • Lesson 6: wavelength and polarization
  • Lesson 7: the dipole, patterns and gain in dBi, height above ground
  • Lesson 8: SWR and antenna analyzers; lesson 2: dB and dBm
  • Lesson 11: thermal noise and noise figure, and the link budget

Lesson 7 explains what an antenna is: a wire whose current launches a wave, and catches one the same way. This lab is about doing antenna work: how much signal an antenna can catch, whether a link will work before you build it, what to cut and how to trim it, where to put it, and how to recognize the antennas you see on rooftops, towers and phones. Every number on this page comes from a formula you can see, checked against textbook values in src/ham/antenna-math.test.ts.

What an antenna catches is an area

A passing radio wave carries its power spread over space, so many watts (or rather, picowatts) per square meter. An antenna collects the power falling on a certain area of that wave, its effective aperture. It isn't the metal's size: a thin wire catches the power of an area far wider than itself.

Predict: the same dipole design, built for 1375 MHz instead of 137.5 MHz (ten times the frequency). How much of a passing wave's power does it collect?

  • The same
  • 10 times less
  • 100 times less
A dipole's aperture scales with the wavelength squared. Ten times the frequency is a tenth of the wavelength and a hundredth of the area: 20 dB less signal from the same wave. That's why high frequencies need gain.

The yellow square is the area the antenna collects, drawn to scale next to a person 1.8 m tall. At 137.5 MHz a dipole collects 0.62 m², about a large window. At 1.37 GHz the same design collects 100 times less, so a GPS or satellite-TV receiver uses gain (a patch, a dish) to get its area back. Gain and area are the same thing seen two ways: doubling the area is +3 dB.

An antenna collects the wave's power over an area: its gain times the wavelength squared, divided by 4π. Low frequencies come with big areas for free; high frequencies have to buy them with gain.

Where it breaks

The aperture formula is for an antenna matched to its feed line and without losses. A short, loaded whip has nearly the same pattern, and so nearly the same aperture, as a full-size dipole, but most of what it collects heats its loading coil instead (lesson 7: efficiency). On the lower HF bands that's often acceptable for receiving, because the sky's noise is collected just as poorly and the signal-to-noise ratio survives; at VHF and above, where the receiver's own noise dominates, the loss counts in full.

Longer wires: more lobes

Lesson 7 says that as a wire gets longer, more lobes form, swinging toward the wire's ends. Here is that sentence as a picture: a straight wire fed in the middle, seen from the side, with its length in wavelengths.

  • Up to a half wave, one figure-eight broadside to the wire: 1.76 dBi for a very short wire, 2.15 dBi at a half wave.
  • Toward a full wave, the figure-eight gets narrower and stronger: 3.82 dBi. At 1.25 waves (the "extended double Zepp" of lesson 7's field guide) it peaks at 5.16 dBi with small side lobes.
  • Beyond, the current along the wire reverses in places, the pattern breaks into more lobes, and the strongest ones swing toward the wire's ends: the long-wire antenna.

The feed-point impedance changes along with it (lesson 7), so a wire that isn't a half wave long usually needs a tuner or a matching section.

Will it get through? The link budget, with the numbers

Lesson 11 adds it up in decibels: transmitter power, plus both antennas' gains, minus losses, minus the path loss, gives the received level. Where does the path loss come from? From the aperture above. The transmitted power spreads over a sphere that grows with the distance squared, and the receiving antenna catches only its aperture's share, which shrinks with the wavelength squared. Together that's the free-space path loss: 20 dB more for every 10× the distance and for every 10× the frequency. Then compare the received level with the receiver's noise floor (lesson 11: −174 dBm per hertz, plus 10·log10 of the bandwidth, plus the noise figure):

Free space is the best case: the formula assumes nothing in the way. Real paths add losses for ground, terrain, buildings and foliage (lesson 9), often tens of dB at VHF in a city, which is why the presets leave you a large margin to play with. Two lessons from the numbers. First, every 6 dB of extra margin doubles the free-space range. Second, the receiver's side counts as much as the transmitter's: 3 dB more antenna gain at your end is the same as doubling their power.

The satellite preset (an example power, not any satellite's published figure) shows why weather satellites are hard. The path loss straight overhead at 830 km is 133.6 dB; at the horizon, 2 500 km or more away, about 10 dB worse. A real satellite also doesn't use free space alone: its signal is right-hand circular (next sections), and your antenna's sky view decides which part of the pass you hear at all.

Why 20 dB per decade of distance and of frequency?
  1. An isotropic transmitter spreads P watts over a sphere of area 4πd², so the power per square meter at distance d is P / (4πd²).
  2. An isotropic receiving antenna collects an area λ² / (4π) (section 1, with gain 1).
  3. Received power = P · λ² / (4πd)², so the loss is (4πd / λ)².
  4. In dB that's 20·log10(4πd/λ): doubling d or halving λ (doubling the frequency) adds 6 dB; 10× adds 20 dB.
  5. With d in km and f in MHz, the constants collect into the familiar 20·log10 d + 20·log10 f + 32.45.

The loss grows with frequency only because the receiving antenna's area shrinks. Two dishes of fixed size do the opposite: their gains grow with frequency faster than the path loss does.

Clearing the path: the Fresnel zone

A line of sight isn't a line. The wave travels in a fat, football-shaped region around it, the first Fresnel zone, and an obstacle that pokes into it takes signal away even if you can still see the far antenna. It's widest in the middle of the path and grows with distance and wavelength:

The usual rule of thumb is to keep at least 60% of the first zone's radius clear of buildings, trees and the ground itself. On a 10 km link at 2.4 GHz the zone is 17.7 m in radius at the middle, which is why point-to-point links sit on masts even when the far end looks visible from the roof.

Polarization: lining up, and going circular

Lesson 6 shows that two linear antennas at right angles hear each other badly. The loss follows the angle between them: nothing when aligned, 3 dB at 45°, and in theory everything at 90° (in practice, reflections leave you 20 dB or more down). Satellites have a problem with that: as a satellite tumbles and the wave crosses the ionosphere, its polarization angle changes on the way, so a linear signal would fade in and out. So many satellites send circular polarization: the field's direction spins as the wave travels. Meteor-M's picture downlink is right-hand circular (WMO OSCAR).

A linear antenna receiving a circular wave always loses exactly half the power, 3 dB, but never more, whatever the angle: a steady small loss instead of deep fades. That's the trade the 137 MHz V-dipole makes. A circular antenna of the right hand (a crossed dipole fed 90° apart, a quadrifilar helix, a helix) gets those 3 dB back; one of the wrong hand loses a great deal. Reflections from buildings flip the hand, which is one reason a satellite signal arriving straight from the sky beats one bounced off a wall.

Building: start long, then trim

No formula gives the exact length of a real antenna: the wire's thickness, insulation, nearby objects and the ground all shift its resonance by a few percent. So the craft is the same for every antenna: cut a little long, measure where it resonates (an antenna analyzer or a NanoVNA, lesson 8), and trim. Starting lengths for some common builds:

These all start from a quarter wavelength and take off about 5% for the "end effect" (lesson 7's 468 rule, in meters). Then trim. Shortening raises the resonant frequency, lengthening lowers it, in proportion: a wire that resonates 2% too high needs to be 2% longer.

Trim in two or three steps, not one: the proportion is exact for a simple wire, but feed lines, insulation and nearby objects make the second measurement worth taking. And measure the antenna where it will live: a dipole tuned on the lawn shifts when it goes up the mast.

Cut a quarter wavelength minus about 5% per element, measure where it resonates, and scale its length by measured ÷ wanted. Repeat once.

Feeding it: the cable, the balun, the choke

What happens between the antenna and the radio decides as much as the antenna does. Three practical rules, each explained in depth elsewhere:

  • Mind the loss. Coax loss grows with frequency and length (lesson 8). On receive, a lossy cable in front of the first amplifier adds its whole loss to the noise figure, which is why weak-signal VHF and UHF stations put the preamplifier at the antenna (noise-figure lab; course chapter 12 shows it measured).
  • Stop the shield from becoming antenna. A balanced antenna (a dipole) on unbalanced coax lets current run down the outside of the shield, distorting the pattern and picking up household noise. A choke at the feed point stops it (workshop lab).
  • Keep water out. Seal outdoor connectors; water in coax raises its loss year by year (lesson 8).

Where to put it

The same antenna can work brilliantly or not at all depending on where it is. In rough order of importance:

  • Clear sky in the directions you need. At VHF and above, signals travel nearly in straight lines (lesson 9), and walls, balconies and roofs above the antenna block or weaken them. This site's own 137 MHz antenna on a north-facing balcony is a measured example: over its first four satellite passes in October 2026 it heard signal only with the satellite to the north at about 25° to 55° above the horizon; overhead and the southern sky, hidden by the building and the balcony above, gave next to nothing (a fifth pass, after an amplifier was added, also heard the south-southwest faintly, low in the sky).
  • Away from metal. Metal within about a quarter wavelength (55 cm at 137 MHz, 51 cm at 146 MHz) detunes an antenna and bends its pattern: railings, gutters, window frames, a metal table.
  • Away from noise. In a city, much of what you hear on receive is electrical noise from nearby devices (lesson 11). Moving an antenna a few meters away from a building's wiring can lower the noise more than any amplifier can raise the signal.
  • Height helps on VHF/UHF (a farther horizon, fewer obstacles) and shapes the pattern on HF (lesson 7: the ground matters).
Where it breaks: "a reflector under the antenna"

A large metal sheet a quarter wavelength below a horizontal dipole sends the dipole's downward half back up. Computed for a perfect, very large reflector (antenna-math.ts): 7.5 dBi straight up instead of the dipole's 2.15, 4.5 dBi at 30° elevation, about the same as the dipole alone at 20°, and 6 dB worse at 10°. So it helps when the signal comes from high in the sky and nothing up there blocks it, and hurts near the horizon. Two things break it in practice. A reflector much smaller than the antenna (a small table under a 1 m dipole) reflects little and mostly detunes it; and one closer than about a tenth of a wavelength drags the feed impedance down and the efficiency with it. A concrete floor or balcony slab below the antenna is already a lossy, uncontrolled reflector of this kind.

Beyond ham radio: a field guide

Every antenna you see is one of a few ideas from this page and lesson 7, tuned for a job.

Dishes, solid and grid

A dish is a curved mirror that collects a big area of the wave and focuses it onto a small antenna, the feed, at its focal point. Its gain is set by how many wavelengths across it is: twice the diameter or twice the frequency, four times the area, +6 dB, and a beam half as wide.

A grid dish is a dish made of parallel rods instead of a solid sheet. To a wave whose electric field runs along the rods, rods spaced much closer than a wavelength look like solid metal, so the grid reflects it just as well while letting wind through. To a wave whose field runs across the rods, the grid is nearly invisible. So a grid dish works for one polarization only, set by the feed, and the rods must be mounted parallel to it. The common "grid antenna" for 900 MHz cellular or 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi is exactly this.

A satellite TV dish is usually an offset dish: a slice of a larger parabola, with the feed below the beam so it doesn't block it. That's why it seems to point at the ground.

Safety first

Antenna work is where hams get hurt. Never put up an antenna where it could fall onto, or be blown into, a power line; use a ground and a lightning arrestor for outdoor antennas; don't climb towers alone; and keep people out of the strong field close to a transmitting antenna (lesson 14).

Check yourself

Why does a GPS receiver need a patch with gain, when a simple dipole is fine at 137 MHz?

A dipole's capture area shrinks with the wavelength squared. At 1575 MHz it's about 130 times smaller than at 137 MHz, so the receiver needs gain to collect enough of the wave.

You double the distance of a free-space link. How much signal do you lose, and how could you win it back?

6 dB. Four times the transmitter power, or 3 dB more gain at each end, or 6 dB more at one.

Your V-dipole hears a circular satellite. What do you lose, and what would get it back?

3 dB, all the time, at any angle. A circular antenna of the same hand (a QFH or crossed dipoles) recovers it.

Your 2 m antenna resonates at 150 MHz instead of 146. Longer or shorter, and by how much?

Longer, by 150 ÷ 146 ≈ 1.027: about 2.7%. Then measure again.

A grid dish gives almost nothing after you mounted it. What's the first thing to check?

Its rods' direction: a grid reflects only the polarization parallel to its rods, so the rods must run the same way as the feed's polarization.
