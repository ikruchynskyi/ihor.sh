Two hands-on ideas: a feed line that radiates when it shouldn't, and the method that finds a fault in any radio.

Baluns and common-mode current

Coax works by keeping two equal and opposite currents together: one on the center conductor, one on the inside of the shield. Their fields cancel outside, so the cable doesn't radiate (↩ lesson 8). But a dipole is balanced (symmetrical) and coax is unbalanced: at the feed point, the outside of the shield is a third path, connected to one half of the antenna. Some current takes it, the common-mode current, and the feed line itself becomes part of the antenna. Make the setup lopsided (a slanting feed line, an object near one half) and see what happens:

Common-mode current on the feed line distorts the antenna's pattern, brings RF into the shack (hot microphones, RF burns, ↩ interference), and picks up household noise on receive. A choke balun (coax wound through ferrite cores, or coiled) presents a high impedance to current on the outside of the shield while leaving the inside current untouched. Ferrite beads on other cables do the same job for interference (↩ lesson 3).

Troubleshooting: find the dead stage

This receiver has gone silent. One stage has failed. You have a signal generator on the antenna input and an oscilloscope probe: click a stage to check whether the signal is still good at its output. Find the fault in as few probes as you can.

The efficient strategy is half-splitting: probe the middle of the chain. If the signal is good there, the fault is after it; if not, before it. Each probe halves the suspects, so 8 stages need at most 3 probes. It's the same idea as a binary search, and it works on any system that's a chain: a receiver, a feed line with connectors, a power distribution. Before reaching for a probe, though, the professional order is: check the obvious (power, cables, settings), look for burned parts and loose connectors, and know what the signal should look like at each point (↩ the receiver chain).
