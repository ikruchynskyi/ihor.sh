Signals and modes

Before you startThis lesson builds on the receiver course, which explains the modes from the inside:
  • Course ch. 9: FM and ch. 10: AM, SSB and Morse
  • Course ch. 4: the spectrum, and ch. 5: mixing

A plain, steady radio wave (a carrier) carries no information. To send something, you change it: its strength, its frequency or its timing (phase). That change is modulation, and every way of doing it has a different footprint on the dial: its bandwidth. Knowing the footprints tells you which mode fits where, why a narrow filter improves reception, and when your signal is spilling onto your neighbors.

The modes and their footprints

Each signal below is generated here, sample by sample, and run through the same spectrum code as the course (↩ ch. 4). The horizontal axis is ±12 kHz around the carrier:

  • CW (continuous wave: Morse code, keyed on and off) is the narrowest of all, around 150 Hz at normal speeds.
  • SSB is a form of amplitude modulation with the carrier and one sideband removed (↩ course ch. 10): about 3 kHz, the narrowest voice mode. That's why it's used for weak-signal, long-distance voice on VHF/UHF. Below 10 MHz, hams conventionally use lower sideband; on 20 meters and up, including 10 meters, VHF and UHF, upper sideband.
  • AM sends the carrier plus both sidebands: about 6 kHz for voice. AM varies the instantaneous power of the signal; its envelope is the outline you get by connecting the peaks.
  • FM varies the instantaneous frequency; PM (phase modulation) varies the phase angle, and sounds the same once received. FM voice on VHF repeaters takes 10 to 15 kHz. Its catch compared with SSB: besides being wider, a receiver only hears the strongest of several signals on a channel at once (the capture effect).
  • Analog fast-scan TV is enormous: about 6 MHz.

Matching your receiver's filter to the mode's bandwidth gives the best signal-to-noise ratio: any wider and you let in noise with no signal in it (↩ course ch. 6: filters).

FM deviation, modulation index and bandwidth

An FM signal swings above and below its center frequency; the size of the swing is the deviation. You might expect its bandwidth to be just twice the deviation, but swinging creates sidebands spaced at the audio frequency, and a useful rule (Carson's rule) says the bandwidth is about 2 × (deviation + highest audio frequency):

  • The modulation index is deviation ÷ modulating frequency (for any one tone). The deviation ratio is the same for the whole system: maximum deviation ÷ highest audio frequency. ±5 kHz with 3 kHz audio: 5 ÷ 3 = 1.67. For phase modulation, the index doesn't depend on the carrier frequency.
  • VHF FM transmitters often generate their signal at a lower frequency and multiply it up with a multiplier stage, which multiplies the deviation too. A 146.52 MHz transmitter built around a 12.21 MHz oscillator multiplies by 12, so for 5 kHz deviation the oscillator only needs to swing 5000 ÷ 12 = 416.7 Hz. A reactance modulator on an RF stage produces phase modulation.

Splatter, key clicks and overmodulation

A clean signal stays inside its footprint. Push it too hard and it spills onto neighboring frequencies:

  • CW: switching the carrier on and off too abruptly (very short rise and fall times) creates key clicks, splatter far from your frequency. The cure is to soften the keying edges. A CW signal's bandwidth depends on the keying speed and the shape of its edges; roughly 4 Hz per word per minute, so 13 WPM needs about 52 Hz.
  • AM/SSB: too much drive or microphone gain causes overmodulation and flat-topping (the peaks are clipped): distortion and excessive bandwidth.
  • Digital: excessive transmit audio into the radio overdrives it, creating intermodulation distortion (IMD) products: on a waterfall, extra vertical lines either side of the signal. For an idling PSK signal, −30 dB IMD is the acceptable maximum.
  • Know your mode's duty cycle: FM and many digital modes transmit at full power 100% of the time, unlike speech, and can exceed a transmitter's average power rating.

Mixing and intermodulation

A mixer multiplies two signals. Its output contains their sum and difference frequencies: that's heterodyning, the heart of every superhet receiver (↩ course ch. 12). The local oscillator is the input you tune, to bring each station to the fixed IF. A station at twice the IF away from the one you want lands on the IF too: the image response.

Any circuit that isn't perfectly linear mixes too, whether you want it or not. Two strong signals in an overloaded amplifier produce intermodulation products:

The troublemakers are the odd-order products such as 2F1 − F2 and 2F2 − F1: they fall right next to the original signals, where no filter can remove them. (Even-order products like F1 + F2 land far away.)

Digital modes: bits on a carrier

To send bits, a transmitter switches between a few distinct states, each called a symbol. Shown as dots on the arrow plane (↩ course ch. 2), the states form a constellation diagram: the possible phase and amplitude of each symbol. Add noise and see when they start to blur:

  • FSK (frequency shift keying) switches between two frequencies, called mark and space; "direct" FSK changes the oscillator's frequency with the data. AFSK does it with audio tones fed into an SSB or FM transmitter. FT8 uses 8-tone FSK in a 50 Hz bandwidth.
  • PSK (phase shift keying) changes the phase. QPSK uses 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° to send pairs of bits. QAM varies the amplitude of two carriers 90° apart (the I and Q of the course!), so each symbol can be one of many points.
  • The symbol rate (the rate at which the waveform changes) is the same thing as baud. A higher symbol rate needs more bandwidth; to send more data without more bandwidth, use a more efficient code (more bits per symbol). Gray code numbers the points so neighbors differ by one bit, so a slight error costs only one bit.
  • Changing phase gently matters: PSK31 shapes its symbols with sinusoidal pulses and changes phase at the RF zero crossing, to stay narrow.

Codes for text: Baudot (RTTY) uses 5 bits per character with start and stop bits, and two shift codes to switch between letters and figures. ASCII uses 7 or 8 bits and allows upper and lower case; an added parity bit lets some errors be detected. PSK31 uses Varicode, where common characters get short codes: lowercase is quick, and capitals take longer.

Fixing errors: forward error correction (FEC) sends redundant information so the receiver can repair errors itself (a coding rate of 3/4 means a quarter of the data is FEC). ARQ (automatic repeat request) has the receiver check each packet and answer NAK to request a retransmission; after too many failed attempts the connection is dropped.

Sharing a channel: frequency division multiplexing gives each data stream its own frequency band; time division multiplexing gives each its own time slots (DMR fits two voice signals in one 12.5 kHz channel this way). OFDM sends data on many subcarriers spaced exactly so they don't interfere. Spread spectrum spreads a signal over a wide band, by frequency hopping (jumping around in a pseudorandom sequence) or direct sequence (a fast bit stream shifting the carrier's phase); the receiver, knowing the sequence, collects it, while ordinary signals are suppressed.

A tour of the digital modes

  • FT8 (in the WSJT-X suite, alongside modes for meteor scatter, moonbounce and weak signals): works far below the noise. Its signal reports are SNR in a 2.5 kHz bandwidth, so "+3" means 3 dB above the noise in that bandwidth. WSPR is a low-power beacon mode for testing propagation.
  • Packet radio (usually FM or PM on VHF): each frame has a header with routing and handling information, plus a checksum. APRS uses packet to put stations, weather and messages on a map in real time.
  • Digital voice: DMR, D-STAR and System Fusion. Mesh networks: modified commercial Wi-Fi equipment forming a ham data network, where each node has an IP address, finds its neighbors by discovery protocols, and routes around a failed node.
  • On a waterfall display, frequency runs across, time runs down, and brightness is signal strength (↩ how this site draws one).

Television, fast and slow

  • NTSC is the analog fast-scan color TV standard of North America: 525 lines per frame, sent interlaced (odd lines in one field, even lines in the next). It uses vestigial sideband: AM with one complete sideband and a sliver of the other, which saves bandwidth while keeping the low video frequencies faithful. Hams send fast-scan TV on 70 cm, on frequencies that match cable TV channels so ordinary TVs can receive it. Digital amateur TV (DVB-T) uses QAM and QPSK.
  • Slow-scan TV (SSTV) sends a still picture as audio over a voice channel: tone frequency encodes brightness, specific tones mark the start of each line, color lines are sent one after another, and a VIS code at the start identifies the mode. Digital SSTV (via DRM) decodes from an SSB receiver. The ISS regularly transmits SSTV pictures you can receive with a cheap dongle.

Link budget, to close: the transmit power plus antenna gains minus all losses, as seen at the receiver. The link margin is how far that received level is above the minimum the receiver needs.

Exam questions, explained

G8B06: Bandwidth of FM with 5 kHz deviation and 3 kHz audio? 16 kHz.

Carson's rule: 2 × (5 + 3) = 16 kHz. Set the FM scene to these values.

E8B03: Modulation index with ±3000 Hz deviation and 1000 Hz audio? 3.

3000 ÷ 1000.

G8B13: Which is an odd-order IMD product of F1 and F2? 2F1 − F2.

2 + 1 = 3, an odd order. It lands close to F1 and F2.

E8D05: How do you reduce key clicks? Increase the keying waveform's rise and fall times.

Softer edges, narrower spectrum: move the CW slider in the splatter scene.

G8A12: What is QPSK? 0°, 90°, 180°, 270° phase shifts, each representing a pair of bits.

Four points on the constellation, two bits each.

E8C07: Bandwidth of 9600-baud ASCII FM with a 4800 Hz shift? 15.36 kHz.

The standard (ITU/FCC) necessary-bandwidth formula for frequency-shift keying: baud rate + 1.2 × shift = 9600 + 1.2 × 4800 = 15 360 Hz.

Check yourself

Why is SSB better than FM for weak-signal VHF contacts?

It concentrates the power in a 3 kHz footprint instead of 10–15 kHz, so the receiver can use a narrow filter and let in less noise.

Your PSK31 signal shows extra vertical lines on the waterfall. What's wrong?

Overmodulation: the transmit audio is too high, producing IMD. Turn it down.

Why are odd-order intermod products worse than even-order ones?

They land right next to the signals that created them (inside your passband), while even-order ones land far away where filters remove them.
