Capacitors and inductors

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 1: Ohm's law, series and parallel, AC
  • Lesson 2: prefixes (micro, pico, milli)
  • For the impedance arrow: course ch. 2, arrows as two numbers

A resistor is simple: it turns current into heat, the same way at every frequency. Radio needs parts with more personality. A capacitor stores energy in an electric field; an inductor stores it in a magnetic field. Both resist change, in opposite ways, and that's why their behaviour depends on frequency. That frequency dependence is what lets a radio pick one station out of thousands.

A capacitor: a tank that fills up

A capacitor is two conductive surfaces (plates) separated by an insulator. No current can cross the gap. But connect a battery and electrons pile up on one plate while the other plate gives electrons away: charge is stored, held by the electric field across the gap. Flip the switch and watch it fill through a resistor:

At first the empty capacitor gulps current as if it were a short circuit. As it fills, its own voltage pushes back against the battery, and the current slows to a trickle. The voltage climbs along a curve that's fast at first and then levels off. That's the water tank picture: filling a tank through a narrow pipe is quick when it's empty and slow when it's nearly full.

How fast? After one time constant, the capacitor has charged to 63.2% of the battery voltage (or, when discharging, dropped to 36.8%). After five time constants, it's essentially full. The time constant is simply R × C: ohms times farads gives seconds. A bigger tank (more capacitance) or a narrower pipe (more resistance) both make it slower.

Capacitance is measured in farads (F). A farad is enormous, so real capacitors are in microfarads (µF), nanofarads (nF) and picofarads (pF). Two kinds you'll meet: electrolytic capacitors pack a lot of capacitance into a small can (for power supplies), and ceramic ones are cheap and small (everywhere else).

An inductor: a flywheel for current

An inductor is a coil of wire. Current through it builds a magnetic field around it, and building that field takes energy. Once built, the field wants to keep the current going. The result is a part that resists any change in current, like a heavy flywheel resists changes in speed:

Close the switch: the current doesn't jump up, it grows, as the field builds (time constant L ÷ R). Open it: the field collapses and tries to keep the current flowing, producing a burst of high voltage, enough to make a spark across the switch. That "kick" is how a car's ignition coil makes 20 000 volts from a 12-volt battery.

Inductance is measured in henries (H); radio coils are usually microhenries (µH). A capacitor stores energy in an electric field; an inductor in a magnetic field. Remember the opposites:

  • A capacitor resists changes in voltage. It passes changes (AC) and blocks steady DC.
  • An inductor resists changes in current. It passes steady DC and fights fast changes (high-frequency AC).

Reactance: opposition that depends on frequency

Feed both parts AC and slide the frequency. On the left, the current that flows; on the right, each part's opposition to AC at every frequency:

This frequency-dependent opposition is called reactance, written X, and measured in ohms like resistance:

  • An inductor's reactance rises with frequency: the faster the current tries to change, the harder the flywheel fights.
  • A capacitor's reactance falls with frequency: at high frequency, the tank never gets the chance to fill up and push back.

Where the two curves cross, their oppositions are equal. That crossing is resonance, the heart of every tuned circuit, and the subject of lesson 4.

Out of step: phase, and the impedance arrow

Reactance has one more twist: it puts current and voltage out of step. In a capacitor, current flows hardest while the tank is empty, so the current peaks a quarter cycle before the voltage: current leads voltage by 90°. In an inductor it's the other way round: voltage leads current by 90°. (A classic memory aid: "ELI the ICE man": in an inductor, L, voltage E comes before current I; in a capacitor, C, current I comes before E.)

That's why resistance and reactance can't simply be added. Think of them as arrows at right angles (↩ course ch. 2): resistance points sideways, inductive reactance points up, capacitive reactance points down. Their sum is the impedance arrow, Z: its length is the total opposition (still in ohms: the ratio of voltage to current), and its angle is how far voltage and current are out of step.

In a series circuit, the up and down arrows cancel as far as they can: the net reactance is XL − XC. If the inductor wins, the arrow tilts up and voltage leads current; if the capacitor wins, it tilts down and voltage lags. The Extra exam's examples are exactly this picture: XC = 500 Ω, R = 1 kΩ, XL = 250 Ω gives a net 250 Ω downwards against 1000 Ω sideways: an angle of 14°, voltage lagging.

Two more words from the same picture. Admittance is the inverse of impedance (how easily current flows rather than how hard): flip the arrow's length to 1 over it, and its angle to the opposite sign. Its up-down part is called susceptance, written B.

Combining capacitors and inductors

Inductors combine like resistors: in series they add, in parallel they shrink (20 mH + 50 mH in series = 70 mH; three 10 mH in parallel = 3.3 mH). Capacitors do the opposite, because putting them side by side makes one bigger plate: in parallel they add (5 nF + 5 nF + 750 pF = 10.75 nF), in series they shrink (three 100 µF in series = 33.3 µF; 20 µF and 50 µF in series = 14.3 µF, by product over sum). So: to increase capacitance, add a capacitor in parallel; to increase inductance, add an inductor in series.

Transformers: two coils sharing a field

Wind two coils on the same core. AC in the first (the primary) makes a changing magnetic field, and that changing field pushes current in the second (the secondary): mutual inductance. The voltage scales with the number of turns:

Energy can't be created, so if the voltage goes up, the current goes down by the same factor: a step-up transformer's primary carries the bigger current, which is why its primary is wound with thicker wire. Feed a 4:1 step-down transformer backwards (into its secondary) and it becomes a 1:4 step-up: the voltage is multiplied by 4.

Transformers also match impedances: the impedance ratio is the turns ratio squared. To match a 600 Ω antenna to 50 Ω coax, the impedance ratio is 12, so the turns ratio is √12 ≈ 3.5 to 1. (More on why matching matters in lesson 8.)

What's inside the coil

Put a magnetic material inside a coil and it concentrates the field, raising the inductance. How strongly a material does that is its permeability, and it's what sets an inductor's value for a given number of turns. Compare a straight coil with a ring-shaped one:

  • A straight coil's (solenoid's) field spills out around it and can couple into nearby circuits. A toroid's field stays almost entirely inside its ring: that's the main reason radio builders love toroids.
  • Ferrite cores have high permeability, so they need fewer turns for a given inductance. Powdered iron cores are less permeable but keep their magnetic properties more stable with temperature.
  • A solid metal core would have currents swirling inside it (eddy currents) that waste power as heat; cores of thin insulated layers (laminations) prevent that. A brass core does the opposite of iron: it decreases inductance, because eddy currents in it push the field out.
  • Too much current drives a core into saturation: it can't concentrate any more magnetic flux, and the inductance collapses.
  • Every real coil also has a little capacitance between its turns. Above its self-resonant frequency, an inductor actually behaves like a capacitor. The flip side: a wire-wound resistor is a coil too, and its inductance makes it unpredictable at radio frequencies. Ferrite beads slipped over a wire add a little inductance and loss, enough to kill unwanted VHF oscillations.
  • Quartz crystals are piezoelectric: squeeze them and they make a voltage; apply a voltage and they flex. A thin slice of quartz vibrates at one extremely precise frequency, and electrically it behaves like a series R, L and C (the vibration) in parallel with a small capacitor (its electrodes). That's the crystal that sets the pace in your dongle (↩ course ch. 12).

The rest of the parts drawer

  • Potentiometer: a resistor with a sliding contact: an adjustable resistance, the classic volume control.
  • Switches: "pole" is how many separate circuits a switch controls, "throw" is how many positions each can connect to. SPST (single-pole single-throw) is a plain on/off; SPDT switches one circuit between two others.
  • Batteries: nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal-hydride, lithium-ion and lead-acid are rechargeable; carbon-zinc is not. A 12 V lead-acid battery lasts longest if never discharged below about 10.5 V. A battery with low internal resistance can deliver a high current, which a transmitter needs.
  • Fuses are deliberately weak links that melt before a fault can overheat wiring: see lesson 14.

Exam questions, explained

G5A05 / G5A06: How do an inductor and a capacitor react to rising frequency? Inductive reactance increases; capacitive reactance decreases.

The reactance scene: the inductor's flywheel fights faster changes harder; the capacitor's tank has less time to fill and push back.

E5B04: Time constant of two 220 µF capacitors and two 1 MΩ resistors, all in parallel? 220 seconds.

The capacitors in parallel add to 440 µF; the resistors in parallel halve to 0.5 MΩ. τ = 0.5 × 10⁶ × 440 × 10⁻⁶ = 220 s.

E5B09 / E5B10: Phase in a capacitor and in an inductor? Current leads voltage by 90°; voltage leads current by 90°.

ELI the ICE man. A capacitor's current is biggest while it's empty, before its voltage builds; an inductor's voltage is biggest while its current is still building.

E5B11: XC 25 Ω, R 100 Ω, XL 75 Ω in series: phase angle? 27°, voltage leading.

Net reactance 75 − 25 = 50 Ω up (inductive), against 100 Ω sideways. The arrow's angle is about 27°, tilted up, so voltage leads.

G5C06: 500-turn primary, 1500-turn secondary, 120 V AC in? 360 volts.

Three times the turns, three times the voltage.

E6D10: Why a toroid instead of a straight coil? It confines most of the magnetic field within the core.

The core scene: the ring keeps its field inside, so it doesn't couple into neighbours.

Check yourself

Which part blocks DC but passes AC, and which does the opposite?

A capacitor blocks steady DC (no current crosses its gap once it's full) and passes AC. An inductor passes DC freely and opposes AC, more so at higher frequencies.

A 10 kΩ resistor and a 100 µF capacitor: how long until the capacitor reaches 63% of the battery voltage?

One time constant: 10 000 × 0.0001 = 1 second.

Why can't you add a 300 Ω resistance and a 400 Ω reactance to get 700 Ω?

They're at right angles: they put current and voltage out of step in different ways. The total is the length of the arrow (300 sideways, 400 up): 500 Ω.
