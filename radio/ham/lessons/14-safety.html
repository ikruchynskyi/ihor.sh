Safety

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 1: Ohm's law; lesson 7: gain and EIRP; lesson 10: duty cycle

Amateur radio is a safe hobby, as long as you respect three things: the RF energy your antenna radiates, the electricity that powers your station, and gravity, when antennas go up towers. Every license exam has a safety section, and it's the one part worth knowing even if you never sit an exam.

RF exposure: heat, not radioactivity

Radio waves are non-ionizing radiation: unlike X-rays or radioactivity, they don't carry enough energy per photon to break chemical bonds or damage DNA. What strong RF fields can do is heat body tissue, the way a microwave oven heats food. So the FCC sets maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limits, in power per area, and they depend on frequency:

  • The limits are lowest, most restrictive, from 30 to 300 MHz: a human body is roughly the size of a half wavelength there, so it absorbs energy best (it resonates, like an antenna). The body absorbs more RF energy at some frequencies than others; the rate at which it absorbs it is the SAR (specific absorption rate). Of the ham bands, 6 meters (50 MHz) and 2 meters have the lowest limits.
  • There are two sets of limits: controlled (you and your family, who know about it and can control it, averaged over 6 minutes) and uncontrolled (the general public, including your neighbors, averaged over 30 minutes). Below 300 MHz, close to antennas, separate electric (E) and magnetic (H) field limits apply, because the two aren't in a simple relationship there.
  • Microwave bands bring their own hazard: the very high-gain antennas used there concentrate power into intense beams.

How close is too close?

Exposure depends on your power, the antenna's gain, the frequency, the distance, and how much of the time you're actually transmitting. Try a few stations:

  • Duty cycle is the percentage of time a transmitter is actually transmitting during the averaging time; together with the mode's own duty cycle (speech is mostly pauses; FM and FT8 are full power the whole time), it sets the average exposure. Halving the duty cycle halves the average power density, so you may run twice the power for the same exposure: a lower duty cycle permits higher power.
  • The calculation here uses the FCC's standard far-field formula with ground reflection (from OET Bulletin 65). Other ways to evaluate: computer modeling, or measuring with a calibrated field-strength meter and calibrated antenna. All are acceptable.

What the rules require

  • Every station with a time-averaged transmission over about one milliwatt is covered. The station licensee is responsible for making sure no one is exposed above the limits. If your station doesn't meet the simple exemption criteria, perform an evaluation following OET Bulletin 65; the exam treats an 80-meter station as always needing one. Handhelds sold before May 3, 2021 are exempt.
  • If the evaluation shows a problem, you must prevent the exposure: reduce power, raise or relocate antennas, or keep people away. If a directional antenna's main lobe could overexpose a neighbor, make sure it can't be pointed at them while they're there. Indoor antennas: check the limits aren't exceeded in occupied rooms. At your neighbor's, signals must be below the uncontrolled limits.
  • Re-evaluate whenever you change anything in the transmitter or antenna system.
  • At shared sites, every transmitter producing 5% or more of its MPE limit where the total is exceeded shares responsibility.
  • Touching an antenna while it transmits can cause an RF burn.

Electricity: what a few milliamps can do

Current through the body can heat tissue, disrupt the heart's rhythm, and make muscles contract so you can't let go: all of these. As little as tens of milliamps across the chest can be fatal. Mains wiring in the US: black is hot, white is neutral, green or bare is the safety ground. A ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) watches for current leaking where it shouldn't:

  • A GFCI compares the current going out on the hot wire with the current coming back on the neutral. Any difference means current is flowing from the hot wire to ground some other way, perhaps through you, and it cuts the power in a fraction of a second.
  • A fuse or circuit breaker removes power if there's an overload. It goes in series with the hot wire(s) only (in a four-wire 240 V circuit, both hot wires). Never replace a fuse with a bigger one (a 5 A fuse with a 20 A one): the wiring could overheat and start a fire before the fuse blows. Wire size must match: AWG 14 wire for a 15 A breaker, AWG 12 for 20 A. The National Electrical Code covers your station's electrical safety; local electrical codes set grounding requirements.
  • Guard against shock in every way: ground the equipment, use three-wire cords, and use GFCI outlets. A power supply interlock removes dangerous voltages if its cabinet is opened. Just after a power supply is turned off, its filter capacitors can still hold a dangerous charge (↩ lesson 5: bleeder resistors).
  • When measuring high voltages, make sure the meter and its leads are rated for them.
  • Batteries: shorting a 12 V storage battery's terminals can cause burns, fire or an explosion; charging or discharging one too fast can overheat it or make it release gas.
  • Run an emergency generator outdoors in a well-ventilated area (carbon monoxide), and wash your hands after handling lead solder.

Lightning and grounding

  • An outdoor ground rod's main job is to dissipate lightning. The lightning ground system belongs outside the building, and all ground rods and earth connections must be bonded together with heavy wire or strap (otherwise a strike can drive large currents between them, through your equipment).
  • Install lightning arrestors on a grounded panel where the feed lines enter the building.
  • Ground connections should be short and direct, with no sharp bends (lightning is a very fast pulse, and a bend is an inductance it would rather jump across). For a tower: a separate 8-foot ground rod at each leg, bonded to the tower and to each other.

Antennas and towers

  • Power lines are the deadliest hazard: look for them and stay clear. Place any antenna so that if it fell, no part could come within 10 feet of power wires, and never attach antennas to utility poles.
  • Never climb a tower without a helper or observer. Wear a hard hat, safety glasses and a harness that's rated for your weight and within its service life; use 100% tie-off (at least one lanyard always attached), clipped to the tower legs, with a shock-absorbing lanyard attached above your head. Before climbing a tower that carries powered equipment, lock out and tag every circuit feeding it.
  • A crank-up tower must not be climbed unless it's fully retracted or has mechanical safety locks. A safety wire through each turnbuckle keeps vibration from loosening the guy lines.

Exam questions, explained

T0C02: Which band has the lowest MPE? 50 MHz.

It's the only choice inside 30–300 MHz, where the body absorbs RF best.

T0C03: Duty cycle from 100% to 50%: the allowable power density? Increases by a factor of 2.

Exposure is averaged over time: half the time on, half the average for the same peak, so twice the peak is allowed.

T0A08: Where does a fuse go in a 120 V circuit? In series with the hot conductor only.

Fusing the neutral could leave equipment live after the fuse blows.

G0B05: What makes a GFCI trip? Current flowing from a hot wire directly to ground.

The out and back currents no longer match: try it in the GFCI scene.

T0A09: What should be done to all external ground rods? Bond them together with heavy wire or strap.

So that lightning can't create a voltage between separate grounds.
