Waves and wavelength

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 1: alternating current and frequency
  • Course ch. 1: a wave as the shadow of a spinning arrow (helpful, not required)

An alternating current in a wire makes electric and magnetic fields around it that change just as fast. At radio frequencies, those changing fields don't stay put: they break away from the wire and travel outward on their own, at the speed of light. That traveling disturbance is a radio wave, and a wire built to launch or catch it is an antenna.

Two fields, at right angles, at the speed of light

A radio wave is made of two fields that keep each other going: a changing electric field creates a magnetic one, and a changing magnetic field creates an electric one. They're always at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel:

Yellow is the electric field, blue the magnetic field. The direction of the electric field defines the wave's polarization: a vertical antenna launches a vertically polarized wave, a horizontal one a horizontally polarized wave. In empty space, every radio wave, of any frequency, travels at the speed of light: about 300 000 000 meters per second.

Wavelength: 300 divided by megahertz

While one cycle happens at the antenna, the wave has already run ahead by a certain distance. That distance, from one crest to the next, is the wavelength. The faster the cycles, the less time each takes, so the shorter the distance: wavelength gets shorter as frequency rises.

Distance per second divided by cycles per second gives distance per cycle: 300 000 000 m/s ÷ f. Measured in megahertz, the millions cancel, leaving the one formula every ham knows by heart:

λ (meters)=300f (MHz)
  • λ (lambda) is the wavelength. 146 MHz → 300 ÷ 146 ≈ 2 m. 7 MHz → about 43 m.

That's why amateur bands are named by their approximate wavelength: the "2-meter band" is 144–148 MHz, "70 centimeters" is 420–450 MHz, "40 meters" is 7.0–7.3 MHz. And the big ranges of the spectrum have names too: HF (high frequency) is 3–30 MHz, VHF (very high) is 30–300 MHz, UHF (ultra high) is 300–3000 MHz. Each is ten times the one before.

Polarization: matching the antennas

A receiving antenna picks up the part of the electric field that lies along it. Turn it across the wave's polarization and the signal fades away:

On a direct, line-of-sight VHF or UHF path, cross-polarization (one antenna vertical, the other horizontal) costs a lot of signal, often 20 dB or more in practice. So conventions matter: FM and repeaters use vertical antennas (easy on cars and handhelds), while long-distance CW and SSB on VHF/UHF use horizontal. On HF it matters much less: signals bent back by the ionosphere arrive with their polarization twisted into a slowly changing ellipse, so either orientation works.

Multipath: when a signal arrives twice

A signal can reach you directly and also bounced off a building. Where the two arrive in step they add up; where they arrive half a wavelength apart they cancel. Drive the receiver along the street:

  • The strong and weak spots repeat about every half wavelength, so on VHF, moving the antenna a few feet can change the signal enormously.
  • Driving through that pattern, a mobile signal flutters rapidly: hams call it picket fencing.
  • For data, the echoes smear the symbols together and error rates rise.
  • Reflections can also help: if a building blocks the direct path to a repeater, point a directional antenna at something that reflects the signal there.
  • On HF, signals often arrive by several paths through the ionosphere, which shift constantly: that's the cause of irregular fading.

What gets in the way

The shorter the wavelength, the more things in its path matter. At UHF and microwave frequencies, vegetation (wet leaves) absorbs signals, and rain and other precipitation can shorten your range. At 10 and 6 meters, wavelengths of several meters, fog and rain have little effect. And at HF, a whole layer of the upper atmosphere, the ionosphere, can bend signals back to Earth, which is how HF reaches around the world (lesson 9).

Exam questions, explained

T3B06: Formula for frequency to approximate wavelength? Wavelength in meters = 300 ÷ frequency in MHz.

The speed of light (300 million m/s) divided by the frequency (millions of cycles per second).

T3B12: Which frequency travels fastest in free space? All the same.

Every radio wave travels at the speed of light; only the wavelength changes with frequency.

T3A01: Why do VHF signals change a lot when the antenna moves a few feet? Multipath cancels or reinforces signals.

At 146 MHz half a wavelength is about a meter: the multipath scene shows peaks and nulls that close together.

T3B02: What defines a wave's polarization? The orientation of the electric field.

Which is set by how the transmitting antenna is oriented.

Check yourself

What's the wavelength at 28 MHz, and what band is that?

300 ÷ 28 ≈ 10.7 m: the 10-meter band.

Your handheld's signal into a repeater drops when you step a meter to the side. Why?

You moved from a spot where direct and reflected signals add up to one where they partly cancel (multipath). At 2 meters, a half wavelength is about a meter.

Why do FM repeater users mostly use vertical antennas?

Convention, set by what's practical on cars and handhelds; using the same polarization as everyone else avoids cross-polarization loss.
