Receivers and transmitters

A transceiver is a receiver and a transmitter in one box, sharing the antenna, the frequency display and often many circuits. Pressing the PTT (push-to-talk) button, which grounds the PTT line, switches it from receive to transmit. This lesson opens the box: what each stage does, what makes a receiver good, and how a transmitter builds a clean signal.

Sensitivity: how weak a signal can you hear?

A receiver's sensitivity is its ability to detect weak signals. What limits it is noise. Even a perfect receiver at room temperature hears thermal noise: −174 dBm in each hertz of bandwidth. So the wider your filter, the more noise comes in:

  • Noise power is proportional to bandwidth: from 50 Hz to 1000 Hz is 20 times wider, so the noise floor rises by 10 × log₁₀(20) ≈ 13 dB. That's why a filter matched to the mode (about 2400 Hz for SSB, a few hundred Hz for CW) gives the best signal-to-noise ratio.
  • A real receiver adds its own noise. Its noise figure is the ratio, in dB, of the noise it generates to the theoretical minimum. The minimum discernible signal (MDS) is the weakest signal it can detect. −100 dBm is 0.1 picowatts.
  • On the lower HF bands, atmospheric noise from the sky is far stronger than any receiver's own noise. So switching in an attenuator there reduces overload without costing signal-to-noise ratio: the signal and the sky noise drop together. A good HF preamplifier matters most on the quiet upper bands and above.

Selectivity and strong signals

Selectivity is the ability to separate signals close together. The real test of a receiver, though, is a very strong signal right next to a very weak one. Every amplifier and mixer becomes slightly nonlinear when pushed, and two strong signals create intermodulation products (↩ lesson 10) that grow three times faster than the signals themselves:

  • Extend the two lines and they meet at the third-order intercept point (IP3). A receiver with a +40 dBm intercept: two +40 dBm input signals would (theoretically) produce a third-order product as strong as the signals themselves. The higher the IP3, the better the receiver copes with strong signals.
  • Blocking dynamic range: the difference in dB between the noise floor and the signal level that causes 1 dB of gain compression. Poor dynamic range shows up as cross-modulation (a strong station's audio riding on others) and desensitization (a strong nearby signal making the receiver deaf to weak ones).
  • The cures: a preselector or front-end filter rejects strong out-of-band signals; a narrow roofing filter early in the IF keeps strong nearby signals away from later stages; attenuation before the first RF stage; and choosing a high IF makes images easier for the front end to reject. IF shift slides the passband to dodge an adjacent station.
  • Reciprocal mixing: a local oscillator with phase noise (a slightly smeared frequency) mixes that smear with strong nearby signals, creating noise on top of the one you want. The same happens with a noisy master clock in an SDR.
  • An SDR's ADC overloads when the input exceeds its reference voltage (↩ course ch. 3: clipping). In FM, a stronger signal on the same frequency simply suppresses a weaker one: the capture effect.
  • Two repeaters close together can intermodulate in each other's final amplifiers; a properly terminated circulator at the transmitter output stops that.

Amplifier classes: efficiency versus faithfulness

A transistor amplifier can conduct for the whole input cycle or only part of it. Less conduction wastes less power as heat, but distorts the waveform:

  • Class A: conducts 100% of the time, biased halfway between saturation and cutoff. Most faithful, least efficient (25–50%).
  • Class AB (usually push-pull): each device conducts for more than 180° but less than 360°. The usual linear amplifier for SSB.
  • Class C: conducts for well under half the cycle. The most efficient of the classic classes (around 70%+), fine for constant-amplitude signals like FM and CW, where a tuned circuit restores the sine wave. Used on SSB, it causes distortion and splatter.
  • Class D: switches fully on and off. A switch is at saturation or cutoff almost all the time, wasting very little, but its square-wave output needs a filter to remove harmonics.
  • A linear amplifier preserves the input waveform (needed for SSB). Efficiency is RF output power divided by DC input power.

Amplifiers can accidentally oscillate. Neutralization cancels the internal feedback that causes self-oscillation, and parasitic suppressors kill unwanted VHF oscillations. A grounded-grid tube amplifier has a low input impedance. In transistor circuits: an emitter follower (common collector) keeps the output in phase with the input; figure E7-1 shows a common-emitter amplifier, where R1 and R2 are a voltage-divider bias and R3 provides self-bias.

Oscillators and synthesizers

An oscillator generates a signal at a specific frequency. Every sine-wave oscillator is an amplifier and a filter in a feedback loop: the amplifier makes up the losses, the filter (an LC tank, or a crystal) picks the frequency. An LC oscillator's frequency is set by its tank's inductance and capacitance (↩ lesson 4). Three classic circuits: Colpitts (feedback through a capacitive divider), Hartley (a tapped inductor) and Pierce (through a quartz crystal).

  • A VFO (variable frequency oscillator) sets the receive and transmit frequency.
  • Stability: crystals run on their marked frequency only with the specified parallel load capacitance; NP0 capacitors reduce thermal drift; mechanical vibration shifting the frequency is microphonics, cured by mechanically isolating the oscillator. Microwave work uses ovens, GPS-disciplined references and the like (all of the above).
  • A phase-locked loop (PLL) is a servo: a phase detector compares a voltage-controlled oscillator with a stable reference, and a low-pass filter smooths the correction. It synthesizes frequencies and can demodulate FM (↩ course ch. 12, with a calculator).

A direct digital synthesizer (DDS) does it with arithmetic, exactly like the course's NCO (↩ course ch. 5): a phase accumulator adds a tuning word every clock tick, a lookup table of amplitude values turns phase into a sample, a DAC makes a voltage, and a low-pass filter smooths the steps:

A DDS tunes in tiny steps with the stability of its crystal clock. Its main impurities are spurious signals at discrete frequencies, from the rounding in the table and the DAC.

Making and detecting modulation

  • The message before modulation (your voice's audio) occupies the baseband.
  • SSB: a balanced modulator produces double-sideband RF with the carrier suppressed, and a filter selects one sideband. The DSP way: combine signals in quadrature (90° apart) using a Hilbert-transform filter. On receive, a product detector (a mixer with a local carrier) recovers the audio; the RIT or clarifier fine-tunes the receiver alone when a voice sounds too high or low (↩ course ch. 10: the duck problem).
  • FM: a reactance modulator varies a capacitance to produce PM or FM; a pre-emphasis network boosts the treble, and de-emphasis in the receiver restores it (compatibility with phase-modulated transmitters). A frequency discriminator detects FM (↩ course ch. 9). Received slightly off frequency, FM sounds distorted.
  • AM: a diode envelope detector rectifies and filters.
  • A mixer's output holds the two input frequencies plus their sum and difference; overdrive it and it generates spurious products.

Software-defined radio and DSP

This whole site is a software radio, so most of this is in the course already:

  • SDRs work with I and Q signals 90° apart, which lets software create or decode any modulation (↩ ch. 1, ch. 2). Filtering, modulation and demodulation are all done in software.
  • Direct sampling digitizes the RF without mixing it first. The sample rate sets the maximum bandwidth; a signal must be sampled at at least twice its highest frequency (↩ ch. 3). The weakest signal it can resolve, without other noise, depends on the ADC's reference voltage and number of bits: 1 V in 1 mV steps needs 1000 levels, so 10 bits (1024).
  • The FFT converts from the time domain to the frequency domain (↩ ch. 4). Decimation reduces the sample rate by removing samples, after an anti-aliasing filter (↩ ch. 7).
  • A FIR filter's taps are incremental delays, each with a weight; more taps give a sharper response, and a FIR filter can delay all frequencies equally (↩ ch. 6). DSP filters can take almost any bandwidth and shape; an adaptive filter removes noise from SSB audio.
  • Filter vocabulary: insertion loss is the attenuation inside the passband; the cutoff frequency is where the output drops to half power; a band-pass filter's bandwidth is measured between its upper and lower half-power points; ultimate rejection is the most it can reject outside.

At the controls, and when things go wrong

  • Enter frequencies with the keypad or VFO knob; scanning tunes through a range checking for activity; set squelch so the audio is on all the time to hear a very weak FM signal (↩ course ch. 11); selectable filter bandwidths reduce noise and interference.
  • Digital voice: a DMR radio's settings (repeaters and talkgroups) live in a code plug; a group is selected by its ID code; a D-STAR radio needs your call sign programmed before transmitting.
  • Too much microphone gain distorts SSB. Told you're over-deviating on FM? Talk farther from the microphone. Distorted audio can also be RF feeding back into the transmitter: a clip-on ferrite choke on the microphone cable fixes that.
  • Low power output from a solid-state rig? Suspect high SWR (it protects itself, ↩ lesson 8).
  • A neighbor's broadcast radio or TV hearing you usually means their receiver can't reject strong signals outside its band; a filter at the affected receiver's antenna input helps. First, make sure your own station is working properly and doesn't interfere with your own TV on the same channel. With cable TV interference, first check that all the coax connectors are properly installed. A nearby commercial FM station overloading your 2-meter radio: install a band-reject filter. More on interference in lesson 15.

Link budget: will it get through?

Add it up in decibels: transmit power (in dBm) + antenna gains − cable losses − path loss = received level. Compare with what the receiver needs (its MDS plus the signal-to-noise ratio the mode requires), and the difference is the link margin. The Extra exam's example: +40 dBm + 10 dBi − 3 dB − 136 dB = −89 dBm received; the receiver needs −103 + 6 = −97 dBm; margin +8 dB. Another: +40 + 6 + 3 − 100 = −51 dBm.

Check yourself

Why use a 500 Hz filter for CW instead of a 2.4 kHz one?

The CW signal fits in 500 Hz; the extra width only lets in noise (about 7 dB more) and other signals.

Why can't a Class C amplifier be used for SSB?

It only conducts for part of each cycle, so it doesn't preserve the varying amplitude of an SSB signal: distortion and splatter.

What do a PLL and a DDS have in common, and how do they differ?

Both make many precise frequencies from one stable reference. A PLL steers an analog oscillator until its phase follows the reference; a DDS computes the waveform sample by sample.
