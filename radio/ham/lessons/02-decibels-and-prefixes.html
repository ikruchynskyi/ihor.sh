Decibels, prefixes, RMS and PEP

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 1: power (watts = volts × amps) and alternating current

Radio deals in numbers of wildly different sizes. A transmitter puts out 100 watts; the same signal arrives at a receiver across the country as a few millionths of a millionth of a watt. A crystal vibrates 28 800 000 times a second; a capacitor holds 0.000 000 000 1 farads. Radio people have two tools for living with this: prefixes, which keep the digits short, and decibels, which turn huge ratios into small, friendly numbers.

Prefixes: moving the decimal point by threes

Every prefix means "times a thousand" or "divided by a thousand", one more time. Changing the prefix just slides the decimal point three places. Pick a quantity and slide:

The ones you'll meet: giga (G, billion), mega (M, million), kilo (k, thousand), then down: milli (m, thousandth), micro (µ, millionth), nano (n, billionth), pico (p, trillionth). Watch the case: MHz is mega, mA is milli. A one-step move makes the number 1000 times bigger or smaller, so 1.5 A is 1500 mA, 3.525 MHz is 3525 kHz, and 1,000,000 pF is 1 µF (two steps: pico → nano → micro).

Decibels: counting doublings and tenfolds

A decibel (dB) describes a ratio of two powers, not an amount. The trick is that it turns multiplying into adding. Two facts carry almost every exam question:

  • +3 dB ≈ double the power (−3 dB ≈ half).
  • +10 dB = ten times the power (−10 dB = a tenth).

Everything else is built from those. Double twice is 4×, so +6 dB. Ten times ten is 100×, so +20 dB. Drag the two powers below and watch the hops add up:

From 5 W to 10 W is one doubling: +3 dB. From 12 W down to 3 W is two halvings: −6 dB. From 20 W to 200 W is one tenfold: +10 dB. Notice what the scale does: equal steps in dB are equal ratios, wherever you are. Going from 1 to 2 watts is the same 3 dB as going from 500 to 1000 watts.

Losses work the same way backwards. A cable that loses 1 dB delivers 10−0.1 ≈ 0.79 of the power: about 21% is lost (the General exam says 20.6%). A loss of 3 dB throws away half.

dB=10×log10(PtoPfrom)
  • log₁₀ asks "ten to the what?": log₁₀(100) = 2, so 100× is 10 × 2 = 20 dB. log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.3, so 2× is 3 dB.
  • You'll also see dBm: decibels compared with one milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W, −73 dBm ≈ 50 billionths of a milliwatt.

The S-meter: dB on a dial

Most receivers have an S meter that shows received signal strength in "S units" from S1 to S9, then in dB above S9. On a properly calibrated meter, each S unit is 6 dB: four times the power. Slide the signal:

Two consequences you'll be asked about. To move a distant station's reading from S8 to S9, you must quadruple your transmitter power (6 dB): turning 100 W into 400 W buys you one S unit. And a signal "20 dB over S9" is 100 times more powerful than one at S9.

RMS: the AC voltage that heats like DC

An AC voltage keeps changing, so which number is "the" voltage? There are three common answers. The peak is the highest point. The peak-to-peak is from the lowest to the highest, twice the peak. And the RMS value is the one that matters for power: the DC voltage that would heat a resistor exactly as much.

The yellow curve is the voltage. The pink curve is the power it delivers to a resistor at each moment, which goes as voltage squared (lesson 1: P = E²/R), so it's never negative. Its average, the dashed pink line, sits at exactly half of the peak power. A DC voltage that delivers that same average has to be the peak divided by √2: RMS = 0.707 × peak. Your wall outlet's "120 volts" is RMS: its peak is 170 V and its peak-to-peak 339 V.

RMS stands for "root mean square": square the voltage, take the mean, take the square root, exactly what the picture does. A true-RMS meter computes this for any waveform; cheaper meters assume a sine wave and are wrong for anything else.

Peak envelope power, and why voice is "peaky"

A transmitter's output is a radio-frequency wave whose height (its envelope) follows the modulation. Two numbers describe its power: the average power, and the peak envelope power (PEP): the average power during the single strongest cycle, at the very top of the envelope. US amateur power limits are stated in PEP.

  • A steady carrier has a flat envelope: every cycle is as strong as the strongest, so PEP equals the average. The ratio is exactly 1.00.
  • SSB voice is mostly quiet with occasional peaks (speech has pauses and soft sounds). Its PEP is set by the loudest syllable, but its average is far lower: about 2.5 to 1 for unprocessed speech, a ratio set by the characteristics of speech itself.
  • A speech processor lifts the soft parts closer to the peaks. PEP stays the same (the transmitter's limit), but the average power rises, so the voice sounds louder at the other end. Turned up too far, it brings distortion, splatter onto nearby frequencies and loud background noise: all the bad things at once.

To compute PEP from an oscilloscope reading across a dummy load: take the peak-to-peak voltage, halve it to get the peak, multiply by 0.707 for RMS, then P = E²/R. 200 V peak-to-peak on 50 Ω: peak 100 V, RMS 70.7 V, PEP = 70.7² ÷ 50 = 100 W.

Where a sideband sits, and band edges

On SSB, your radio displays the frequency of the (suppressed) carrier, but your signal isn't there: it occupies about 3 kHz to one side of it (↩ course ch. 10: sidebands). Exam questions, explained

T5B10: 12 watts down to 3 watts is how many dB? −6 dB.

12 → 6 → 3 is two halvings, each −3 dB.

T5B08: 1,000,000 picofarads equals? 1 microfarad.

A million is two prefix steps (×1000 twice): pico → nano → micro.

G4D07: S8 to S9 at a distant receiver needs how much more power? About 4 times.

One S unit is 6 dB, two doublings: 2 × 2 = 4.

G5B08: Peak-to-peak voltage of 120 V RMS? 339.4 volts.

Peak = 120 ÷ 0.707 = 169.7 V; peak-to-peak is twice that.

G5B14: PEP of 500 V peak-to-peak across 50 Ω? 625 watts.

Peak 250 V, RMS 176.8 V, and 176.8² ÷ 50 = 625 W.

E8A06: PEP-to-average ratio of unprocessed SSB voice? About 2.5 to 1.

Speech is peaky: the loudest moments set PEP, while the average includes all the soft sounds and pauses.

E8A09: How many levels can an 8-bit converter encode? 256.

Each bit doubles the count: 2⁸ = 256. It's the ruler in course chapter 3.

Check yourself

Your amplifier turns 100 W into 1000 W. How many dB, and how many S units at the other end?

10× is 10 dB, which is a little under two S units (6 dB each).

Why can't you just average an AC voltage to find its heating effect?

Its plain average is zero: it's positive half the time and negative half the time. Power goes as voltage squared, which is always positive, so you average the square and take the root.

Does a speech processor raise PEP?

No. PEP is set by the peaks; the processor raises the average by lifting the softer sounds.
