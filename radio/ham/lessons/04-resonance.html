Resonance and Q

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 3: reactance (the two curves that cross) and the impedance arrow
  • Lesson 2: decibels (for "half power")

Push a child on a swing at just the right moment, and small pushes build a big swing. Push at the wrong rhythm and nothing much happens. That's resonance: a system that has its own natural rhythm responds hugely to that one rhythm and hardly at all to others. A tuning knob on a radio does exactly this, with a capacitor and an inductor instead of a swing.

The tank circuit: energy sloshing back and forth

Charge a capacitor, then connect it across an inductor. The capacitor empties through the coil, building its magnetic field. When the capacitor is empty, the coil's field (the flywheel, ↩ lesson 3) keeps the current going and charges the capacitor up the other way. Then it all happens in reverse. Energy swings between the electric field and the magnetic field, like a swing trading height for speed:

That's a resonant or tuned circuit: an inductor and a capacitor, in series or in parallel. It rings at one frequency, set by how big the "tank" and the "flywheel" are. A bigger capacitor or a bigger coil makes it slower. Its frequency is exactly where the two reactance curves of lesson 3 cross:

f0=12πLC
  • Set XL = XC, that is 2πfL = 1 ÷ (2πfC), and solve for f. Resistance doesn't appear: it changes how long the ringing lasts, not its pitch. 50 µH with 40 pF: 1 ÷ (2π × √(50×10⁻⁶ × 40×10⁻¹²)) = 3.56 MHz.

Pushing the swing: the resonance curve and Q

Now drive the circuit with a signal and sweep its frequency. Only near the natural frequency does the response build up. The resistance in the circuit is friction: it drains energy every cycle, and decides how sharp the peak is.

The sharpness has a name: Q, for "quality". High Q means little loss: the circuit rings for many cycles on its own, the peak is tall and narrow, and it's very selective. Q also tells you the width of the peak directly. The half-power bandwidth (between the two points 3 dB down from the top) is the resonant frequency divided by Q: a 7.1 MHz circuit with a Q of 150 is 7.1 MHz ÷ 150 = 47.3 kHz wide.

The same trade-off appears everywhere: raising the Q of an impedance-matching circuit makes it narrower (less bandwidth). And in a high-Q series circuit, the voltages across the coil and the capacitor individually become much larger than the voltage you apply, Q times larger, because the energy sloshing between them builds up just like the swing.

Series and parallel: opposite personalities

Where the L and C sit relative to the signal changes everything. Sweep the frequency and compare:

  • Series LC: at resonance the coil's and capacitor's reactances are equal and opposite, so they cancel. All that's left is the resistance: the impedance is at its minimum, approximately R, and current and voltage are in phase. A series circuit is a door that opens wide at one frequency.
  • Parallel LC: at resonance, the current sloshing between the coil and capacitor (the circulating current) is at its maximum, but the two branches take turns, so the current drawn from outside is at its minimum. The impedance is at its maximum. A parallel circuit is a wall that rises at one frequency. (Strictly, at resonance both series and parallel circuits look like a pure resistance: small for series, large for parallel.)
  • For a parallel circuit, Q is the resistance divided by the reactance of either the coil or the capacitor (they're equal at resonance).

Impedance on a graph: rectangular and polar

Lesson 3 drew impedance as an arrow: resistance across, reactance up (inductive) or down (capacitive). There are two ways to write that arrow down, exactly like the two languages for arrows in course chapter 2:

  • Rectangular: R ± jX. The "j" marks the reactance, with + for inductive and − for capacitive. So 50 − j25 Ω means 50 Ω of resistance in series with 25 Ω of capacitive reactance, and a pure 100 Ω capacitive reactance is 0 − j100.
  • Polar: magnitude and angle. A pure inductance is at +90°, a pure capacitance at −90°, a pure resistance at 0° (on the horizontal axis).

Graphs of frequency response usually use a logarithmic scale on the vertical axis (decibels), so that both the passband and the deep rejection fit on one picture. A diagram showing impedances as arrows at one frequency is a phasor diagram.

Power that comes back, and wires that aren't ideal

A resistor turns energy into heat for good. An ideal coil or capacitor only borrows energy: it stores it in its field for a quarter cycle and hands it back the next. The current and voltage are 90° apart, and on average no power is used at all. That borrowed-and-returned flow is called reactive power: "wattless", nonproductive power. In a circuit with 100 Ω of resistance and 100 Ω of inductive reactance drawing 1 A, only the resistor consumes anything: I² × R = 100 W.

At radio frequencies, the parts themselves stop being ideal:

  • Skin effect: at high frequencies, current crowds into a thin layer at the surface of a wire, so its effective resistance rises with frequency. It's also the main loss in film capacitors at RF.
  • Every wire is a small inductor: at VHF and above, keep component leads short to minimize their inductive reactance (and, at microwaves, the phase shift along them).
  • Every part has parasitic reactance: electrolytic capacitors have too much inductance to work at RF; coils have capacitance between their turns. A part's self-resonance is where its intended and parasitic reactances resonate together.
  • A thicker conductor is electrically "longer" than its physical length (its electrical length increases with diameter): antenna builders trim thick elements shorter for that reason.

Filters built from L and C

Combine coils and capacitors and you get filters. The rule of thumb from lesson 3 tells you what each does: a series inductor blocks high frequencies, a capacitor to ground drains them away; swap them for a high-pass.

  • A Pi-network low-pass: a capacitor from the input to ground, an inductor in series, a capacitor from the output to ground (the shape of the letter π). It's the classic transmitter output network. Add one more series inductor at the output and it's a Pi-L network, with even better suppression of harmonics.
  • A T-network with series capacitors and a shunt inductor is a high-pass.
  • An impedance-matching network cancels the reactive part of an impedance and transforms the resistive part to the value you need.

Filters with the same number of parts can be tuned for different personalities. Compare three classic designs, all with the same number of parts:

  • Butterworth: perfectly flat in the passband, gentle cutoff.
  • Chebyshev: allows some ripple in the passband in exchange for a sharper cutoff.
  • Elliptic: ripple in both bands and notches in the stopband, for the sharpest cutoff of all.

How sharp a filter is gets summed up by its shape factor: the ratio of its width far down (say −60 dB) to its width near the top (say −6 dB). A perfect brick wall would be 1; the closer to 1, the better it rejects signals in adjacent channels. Some special filters you'll see named: crystal lattice filters (quartz crystals, for very sharp low-level IF filters), helical filters (compact band-pass or notch filters at VHF/UHF), and cavity filters (large, extremely sharp resonators, used in the duplexer that lets a 2-meter repeater transmit and receive at once).

Exam questions, explained

T6D11: Which of these is a resonant circuit? An inductor and a capacitor in series or parallel.

The tank: energy swings between the coil's magnetic field and the capacitor's electric field.

E5A10: Resonant frequency with 50 µH and 10 pF? 7.12 MHz.

1 ÷ (2π √(50×10⁻⁶ × 10×10⁻¹²)) = 7.12 MHz. R doesn't matter for the frequency.

E5A12: Half-power bandwidth at 3.7 MHz with Q = 118? 31.4 kHz.

Bandwidth = f ÷ Q = 3 700 000 ÷ 118 ≈ 31 356 Hz.

E5A06 / E5A07: Parallel LC at resonance: circulating current, and input current? Maximum; minimum.

The coil and capacitor pass energy back and forth between themselves (big current inside the loop), while the source only tops up losses (small current from outside).

E5C06: What does 50 − j25 Ω represent? 50 Ω resistance in series with 25 Ω capacitive reactance.

Rectangular form: the real part is resistance; −j means capacitive.

E5C10: 400 Ω and 38 pF at 14 MHz: which point on E5-1? Point 4.

XC = 1 ÷ (2π × 14 MHz × 38 pF) ≈ 299 Ω, so the arrow is 400 across and about 300 down: set the plane scene to these values.

E7C05: Ripple in the passband and a sharp cutoff? Chebyshev.

The filter-family scene: Chebyshev trades a flat passband for steepness.

Check yourself

You double the capacitance in a tuned circuit. What happens to its frequency?

It drops by a factor of √2 (about 0.71×): frequency goes as one over the square root of L × C.

Why does a high-Q circuit select one station better than a low-Q one?

Its resonance peak is narrower (bandwidth = f ÷ Q), so signals a little off frequency get much less response.

Where does an ideal capacitor's "reactive power" go?

Nowhere: it's stored in the electric field for a quarter cycle and returned the next. Only resistance consumes power.
