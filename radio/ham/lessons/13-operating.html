On the air

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 12: rules and bands; lesson 10: digital modes; lesson 9: propagation

The rules say what you may do; operating practice says how to do it well. Most of it is courtesy and efficiency: listen first, keep it short, make it easy for the other station. This lesson walks through a ham's day: the local repeater, an HF contact, a net, a satellite pass.

Repeaters: borrowing a mountaintop

A repeater sits somewhere high, listens on one frequency (its input) and simultaneously retransmits what it hears on another (its output), extending a handheld's range from a few miles to dozens. The difference between the two is the offset: ±600 kHz on 2 meters, ±5 MHz on 70 cm. Most repeaters also ignore any signal that doesn't carry their access tone. Set up your radio to use this one (output 146.940 MHz, −600 kHz offset, 100.0 Hz tone):

  • The sub-audible tone is CTCSS (continuous tone-coded squelch system): it opens the repeater's squelch. If you can hear a repeater but can't get in, check the offset and the tone (or just that you're out of range): all of these are possible causes.
  • Reverse swaps your radio's frequencies so you listen on the repeater's input: if you can hear the other station there directly, you can move to simplex (both stations on one frequency) and free the repeater. That's why band plans set aside simplex channels; the national 2-meter FM simplex calling frequency is 146.520 MHz.
  • DTMF (the two-tone "touch tone" signals) sends commands to repeaters, such as connecting to IRLP, which links repeaters over the internet. EchoLink lets you transmit through a repeater from a computer or phone (after registering your call sign with proof of license); a station connecting radio to the internet is a gateway. Linked repeater networks retransmit a signal on all their repeaters at once.
  • On a digital (DMR) repeater, the radio's color code is the repeater's access code, and talkgroups (selected by their ID) organize traffic so you only hear the group you joined.

Using a repeater: to call a station you know, say their call sign, then yours. To show you're available, say your call sign followed by "listening". On FM, if your audio drops out on voice peaks, you're talking too loudly; if you're over-deviating, talk farther from the mic.

Say it so it can't be misheard

Over a noisy channel, B, D, E, P, T and V all sound alike. The NATO phonetic alphabet fixes that; use it for call signs and unusual words. Type yours:

And the most common Q signals, three-letter shorthand from the days of Morse (with a question mark, each becomes a question):

CQCalling any station. QRL?Is this frequency in use? (Always ask before calling.) QRMInterference from other stations. QRN: troubled by static (natural noise). QRSSend more slowly. (If someone sends QRS?, they're asking you to slow down: send slower.) QRVI am ready to receive. QSLI have received and understood. (Also the postcard that confirms a contact.) QSYChanging frequency. QRPLow-power operation (usually 5 W or less).

Morse extras: KN at the end of a transmission means "only the station I'm working, go ahead"; AR ends a formal message; a "C" after an RST signal report means chirpy, unstable. Zero beat means matching your transmit frequency exactly to the station you hear. Full break-in (QSK) lets you hear between your own dots and dashes. Answer a CQ no faster than it was sent, and no faster than you can comfortably copy.

An HF contact, step by step

  1. Pick a frequency following the voluntary band plan (it's not law, but everyone uses it: for example, FT8 lives around 14.074 MHz, most 20-meter digital modes between 14.070 and 14.100). No one owns a frequency, except that emergency traffic always has priority.
  2. Listen, then ask: on CW, send QRL? with your call; on phone, "Is this frequency in use?" and your call. Leave room for neighbors: about 150–500 Hz for CW, 2–3 kHz for SSB.
  3. Call CQ: "CQ CQ CQ, this is KF1XXX, KF1XXX", then listen, and repeat. "CQ DX" means the caller wants stations far away (from the lower 48 states: anyone outside them).
  4. Answering: their call sign, then yours. Exchange signal reports early so each side can adjust. To break into a conversation on phone, say your call sign once.
  5. Voice on HF is SSB (narrow and power-efficient): lower sideband on 160, 75/80 and 40 meters, upper sideband on 20 meters and up, on 17 and 12 meters, and on VHF/UHF, purely by long-standing convention. Set the mic gain so the ALC just works. VOX switches to transmit when you speak, for hands-free operation.
  6. If propagation changes and another conversation starts to overlap, sort it out amicably; no one has priority. Keep a log: not required, but it helps if the FCC asks about your station.

Reaching far: a directional antenna for a long-path contact points 180° from the short-path heading. An azimuthal projection map, centered on your location, shows true bearings and distances to everywhere. And there's the Volunteer Monitor Program: hams formally enlisted to monitor for rule violations and encourage self-regulation (for example, by comparing beam headings to find a station holding a repeater on).

Contests and DX

  • Contesting: contacting as many stations as possible in a set time. Send only the minimum: call sign and the contest exchange, still identifying as the rules require. Contests stay off 30 meters (and the other "WARC" bands). In VHF/UHF contests, the activity is in the weak-signal segments near the calling frequencies; with FT8/FT4 there, the grid square replaces the signal report.
  • In a pileup, send your full call sign once or twice. DX stations often transmit and receive on different frequencies (split) to spread the callers out, hear better, and keep their own frequency clear.
  • Logs travel as ADIF files; contest logs are submitted in Cabrillo format; contacts are confirmed through Logbook of The World (LoTW) or paper QSL cards, which a DX station's QSL manager may handle. Operating a remote station located in the US needs no extra indicator after your call. The delay between your action and the transmitter's response is latency.
  • Radio direction finding locates noise sources and jammers; a hidden transmitter hunt ("fox hunt") turns it into a game, with a directional antenna (↩ lesson 7).

Nets, traffic and emergencies

  • A net is an on-air meeting run by a net control station, who calls it to order and directs the stations checking in. Transmit only when directed (unless reporting an emergency). Good nets have a backup frequency.
  • Traffic is formal messages. A radiogram's preamble holds the information to track it; its check is the number of words in the text. Spell unusual words phonetically.
  • ARES (Amateur Radio Emergency Service): licensed volunteers who registered their skills and equipment for public service. RACES is an FCC Part 97 service for civil-defense communications, requiring certification by a civil-defense agency; only FCC-licensed amateurs may be its control operators, and its drills are limited to 1 hour a week without special authorization.
  • Winlink carries email over radio, with addresses based on call signs, through gateway stations (Remote Message Servers), using protocols like VARA and PACTOR. AREDN mesh networks provide high-speed data for emergencies and events.
  • The FCC rules always apply, even in emergencies, but they themselves allow any means of communication, including outside your license privileges, when there's an immediate threat to human life or property and no other means is available. If a station in distress breaks into your contact: stop, acknowledge them, and find out what help is needed.

Working satellites

Amateur satellites and the ISS pass overhead several times a day. A low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite circles the Earth in about 100 minutes and is in view for ten minutes or so. As it approaches, its signal is higher in frequency; as it recedes, lower: the Doppler shift. Watch a pass:

  • A tracking program (fed with Keplerian elements, the numbers describing the orbit) predicts passes, points your antenna, and corrects for Doppler. An ascending pass goes from south to north. A geostationary satellite appears to stay still in the sky.
  • A satellite's mode names its uplink and downlink bands: U/V means up on 70 cm, down on 2 m. "L band" and "S band" are 23 cm and 13 cm.
  • Many satellites use a linear transponder, which relays a whole slice of band (SSB, CW, data, anything); an inverting one mixes it with a local oscillator and transmits the difference, flipping the slice (upper sideband up becomes lower sideband down). Use only enough uplink power: your downlink should be about as strong as the satellite's beacon, which sends telemetry about the satellite's health (anyone may receive it). Too much power takes the transponder's power away from everyone else.
  • A spinning satellite's signal fades as its antennas rotate (spin fading); circularly polarized antennas reduce that and Faraday rotation. Store-and-forward satellites hold digital messages for later download. Technicians and up may work the ISS; satellites carry FM, SSB, CW and data.

Digital modes in practice

  • FT8: 15-second cycles, so your computer clock must be accurate to about a second; answer a CQ on a clear frequency in the alternate time slot; always USB (as are JT65, JT9 and FT4). FT4 uses four tones. These WSJT-X modes decode far below the noise; Q65 (averaging several receive cycles) is designed for moonbounce, MSK144 for meteor scatter; WSPR is a beacon mode with no keyboard chat; FST4 offers several speeds for weak signals.
  • RTTY: FSK with a 170 Hz shift, sent as AFSK on LSB by convention. If you can't decode an apparently well-tuned signal, the shift, the baud rate or the sidebands could be wrong (all of these). Direct FSK keys the transmitter's VFO; AFSK feeds tones into the microphone input.
  • PACTOR links exactly two stations (you can't join a contact) and can transfer binary files; PACTOR IV is the fastest of the common HF modes under good conditions. Interference makes PACTOR or VARA slow down, resend, or drop the link.
  • PSK31 uses variable-length character codes; FT8 has the narrowest bandwidth of the common modes. Data emissions below 30 MHz are generally FSK.
  • APRS uses the AX.25 packet protocol, sending beacons as Unnumbered Information frames, relayed by digipeaters; the path WIDE3-1 asks for three hops with one remaining. APRS tracks balloons in real time.
  • ALE (automatic link establishment) scans a list of frequencies and wakes the radio when its call sign is heard. EME contacts take turns in timed transmissions.

Exam questions, explained

T2A01 / T2A03: Common repeater offsets? ±600 kHz on 2 m; ±5 MHz on 70 cm.

Set the repeater scene to the right offset and tone to get in.

T2B01: What does the reverse function do? Listens on the repeater's input frequency.

If you hear the other station directly there, you can use simplex instead.

T8B07: What is Doppler shift? A change in frequency caused by relative motion between satellite and station.

In the satellite scene, the downlink starts high, crosses zero shift at closest approach, and ends low.

G2A01: Most common voice mode at 14 MHz and up? Upper sideband.

Convention: LSB below 10 MHz, USB above.

E2E06: Length of an FT8 transmission cycle? 15 seconds.

Stations alternate between even and odd 15-second slots, hence the clock accuracy requirement.
