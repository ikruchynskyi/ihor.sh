Ham radio, visually · Lesson 1

Volts, amps and ohms

Before you startNo background needed. This lesson is the foundation for every other one, and for about 70 exam questions across Technician and General.

Every radio, from a handheld walkie-talkie to a kilowatt transmitter, runs on the same three quantities: a push, a flow, and something that resists the flow. Engineers call them voltage, current and resistance. Once you can see them, the formulas on the exam stop being things to memorize: they're just descriptions of what you watched happen.

What flows: electrons

Everything is made of atoms, and atoms have tiny charged particles called electrons around them. In most materials, every electron is tied to its own atom. In metals, it's different: some electrons are loose. They wander freely between the atoms, like a crowd milling around a square.

Below is a piece of copper wire, magnified enormously. The grey circles are atoms; the blue dots are the free electrons. Now connect a battery, and turn up its push:

With no push, the electrons jiggle around but go nowhere on average. Add a push and the whole crowd starts to drift along the wire. That drift is an electric current: a flow of electrons. It's measured in amperes (amps, A): how much charge passes a point each second.

The push itself is voltage, measured in volts (V). Strictly, it's a difference in electrical pressure between two points, like the difference in water pressure between the two ends of a pipe. A battery keeps one terminal at a higher "pressure" than the other, and that difference is what makes electrons move.

Now tick glass. Its electrons are all tied to their atoms: they wiggle in place, and no push you can make here gets them to travel. That's an insulator. Metals are good conductors because they have many free electrons; glass, plastic, rubber and dry air are insulators. That's why wires are copper wrapped in plastic.

A surpriseEach electron drifts astonishingly slowly, often less than a millimeter per second. Yet a lamp lights the instant you flip the switch. How? The wire is already full of electrons; the push travels through the crowd almost at the speed of light, like a shove going down a packed line of people, while each person only shuffles a step.

Push, flow and resistance: Ohm's law

Below, a battery pushes current around a loop through a resistor, a part made to resist the flow (the yellow zigzag). On the right is the same thing as water: the height of the water in the tank is the voltage, the narrowness of the pipe is the resistance, and the stream coming out is the current.

Play with both sliders and two things are obvious:

  • More push, more flow. Double the voltage and the current doubles.
  • More resistance, less flow. Double the resistance and the current halves.

That's all of Ohm's law: current equals voltage divided by resistance. Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω, the Greek letter omega). One volt pushes one amp through one ohm.

In formulas, voltage is written E (for "electromotive force", an old name that the exam still uses) or V, current is I (from the French intensité), and resistance is R. So:

I=ERE=I×RR=EI
  • Three ways of writing the same fact. You only need to remember one: the other two follow by rearranging.

Resistance is a slope

Here's another way to see it. Plot voltage across, current up. For one resistor, every possible voltage gives a current on a straight line through zero. Drag the point:

A steep line means lots of current for little push: a small resistance. A flat line means a big one. Whatever two numbers you know, the third is where the line goes. Try the exam's examples: 3 amps flowing at 90 volts is a line through (90 V, 3 A), and the readout tells you it's 30 Ω.

Pause and ponderA 12-volt source drives 4 amps through a circuit. Where's the point, and what's the resistance? (Answer: (12 V, 4 A), so R = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 Ω.)

Power is a rectangle

Voltage is how hard each bit of charge is pushed; current is how much charge goes by each second. Multiply them and you get how much energy is delivered each second: the power, measured in watts (W). Power is the rate at which energy is used.

Multiplying two numbers is the area of a rectangle. Drag the corner: width is voltage, height is current, and the area is the power that lights the lamp.

A car battery at 13.8 volts supplying 10 amps to a radio: the rectangle is 13.8 by 10, so the radio draws 138 watts. And backwards: to get 120 watts from 12 volts, the rectangle must be 10 amps tall.

Two handy variations come from mixing in Ohm's law. If you know the voltage and the resistance (not the current), the current is E ÷ R, so the power is E × E ÷ R. If you know the current and the resistance, the voltage is I × R, so the power is I × I × R. For example, 400 volts across an 800-ohm load: 400 × 400 ÷ 800 = 200 watts.

P=I×E=E2R=I2×R
  • P in watts, I in amps, E in volts, R in ohms. The last two are the first one with Ohm's law plugged in.

Series and parallel

Two resistors can be connected in two basic ways: one after the other, so the same current must go through both (series), or side by side, so the current splits between them (parallel). Switch between them:

  • Series: there's only one path, so the current is the same through every part. The battery's voltage is shared out: each resistor "uses up" a slice proportional to its resistance. The resistances simply add: 10 Ω + 20 Ω = 30 Ω.
  • Parallel: each resistor is connected straight across the battery, so the voltage is the same across every part. The currents through the branches add up to the total. And because there are now two paths, the total resistance is less than the smallest one: 10 Ω and 20 Ω in parallel make about 6.7 Ω.

For parallel resistors, add up their "easiness" (one divided by each resistance) and turn the total back into a resistance. For two resistors there's a shortcut: product over sum, (10 × 20) ÷ (10 + 20) ≈ 6.7 Ω. And two equal resistors in parallel make exactly half of one.

Rseries=R1+R2+…1Rparallel=1R1+1R2+…
  • Example from the General exam: 10, 20 and 50 Ω in parallel: 1/10 + 1/20 + 1/50 = 0.17, and 1 ÷ 0.17 ≈ 5.9 Ω.

Direct and alternating current

A battery always pushes the same way: direct current (DC). The outlet in your wall is different: its push reverses direction 60 times a second in North America (50 in most of the world). That's alternating current (AC): current that alternates between positive and negative directions.

With AC, the electrons don't travel around the circuit at all: they rock back and forth in place. Energy still gets delivered, just as a saw cuts wood by going back and forth. The number of complete back-and-forth cycles per second is the frequency, in hertz (Hz). If that wave looks familiar, it's the height of a spinning arrow (↩ course ch. 1): radio signals are alternating currents too, just millions of times faster: kilohertz (kHz, thousands), megahertz (MHz, millions).

Resistance opposes current of every kind: direct, alternating, and the radio-frequency kind alike. Capacitors and inductors (lesson 3) are different: their opposition depends on the frequency, which is what makes tuning possible.

Measuring: how meters connect

A voltmeter measures the push between two points, so it's connected across the part, in parallel. An ammeter measures the flow through a wire, so the circuit has to be opened and the meter put in series, so the current goes through it. A multimeter does both (and resistance), depending on its setting. Try each connection, including the two classic mistakes:

The reason the mistakes go wrong: a voltmeter is built with a huge resistance, so that connecting it across something barely disturbs the circuit. An ammeter is built with a tiny resistance, so that putting it in the path barely changes the current. Swap their places, and the voltmeter in series blocks the current, while the ammeter across the battery becomes a near short circuit, blowing its fuse or worse.

An ohmmeter (a multimeter on its resistance setting) works by sending a small current of its own through the part and measuring the voltage that results: Ohm's law again. Two consequences:

  • Only measure resistance on a circuit with the power off: the circuit's own voltages would confuse the reading, and trying to measure a live voltage on the resistance setting can damage the meter.
  • Connected across a large, empty capacitor, an ohmmeter shows a resistance that increases with time: at first the capacitor soaks up current like a short circuit, then less and less as it fills (lesson 3).

A note on soldering, which the Technician exam groups with meters: use rosin-core solder for electronics, never acid-core (made for plumbing; the acid eats circuits). A good joint is smooth and shiny; a cold joint, where the parts weren't heated enough, looks rough or lumpy and may not conduct reliably.

Exam questions, explained

T5A05: A difference in which of the following causes electron flow? Voltage.

Voltage is a difference in electrical pressure between two points; electrons drift from one to the other, like water from high to low (the first scene).

T5D07: Current with 120 V across 80 Ω? 1.5 amperes.

I = E ÷ R = 120 ÷ 80 = 1.5 A. On the slope picture: the line for 80 Ω, at 120 V.

T5D13 / T5D14: Where is the current always the same? The voltage? Series; parallel.

In series there's one path, so the same current goes through everything. In parallel, every part is connected directly across the same two points, so they all see the same voltage.

T5C11: Current needed for 120 W at 12 V DC? 10 amperes.

Power is the rectangle's area: 120 = 12 × I, so I = 10 A.

G5B05: Power with 7.0 mA through 1,250 Ω? About 61 milliwatts.

P = I² × R = 0.007 × 0.007 × 1250 = 0.061 W. (mA is milliamps, thousandths of an amp: lesson 2 covers the prefixes.)

G5C04: 100 Ω and 200 Ω in parallel? About 67 ohms.

Product over sum: 100 × 200 ÷ 300 ≈ 67 Ω. Less than the smaller one, as parallel always is.

T7D02 / T7D03: How do a voltmeter and an ammeter connect? In parallel; in series.

A voltmeter compares two points, so it goes across the part. An ammeter counts what flows through a wire, so it goes in the path.

Check yourself

Why do metals conduct electricity and glass doesn't?

Metals have many free electrons that can drift when pushed; in glass, every electron is bound to its atom.

You double the voltage across a resistor. What happens to the current, and to the power?

The current doubles (Ohm's law). The power quadruples: the rectangle is twice as wide and twice as tall.

Three 30 Ω resistors: what's their total in series, and in parallel?

Series: 90 Ω. Parallel: 10 Ω (three equal paths, each taking a third of the current).

Why must an ohmmeter be used only on an unpowered circuit?

It measures resistance by applying its own small current and reading the voltage. Any voltage already in the circuit corrupts the reading, and can damage the meter.
