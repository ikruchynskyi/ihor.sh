Diodes, transistors and power supplies

Before you startThis lesson uses:
  • Lesson 1: electrons, conductors and insulators, AC and DC
  • Lesson 3: capacitors (for smoothing) and transformers

Conductors let current through; insulators don't. Semiconductors, like silicon, sit in between, and that's what makes them useful: by adding tiny traces of other elements, engineers decide exactly where and when current may flow. Out of that come diodes, one-way valves for current, and transistors, valves that a small current can open and close. Every radio, power supply and computer is built from them.

Inside a diode: the PN junction

Pure silicon conducts poorly: all its electrons are tied up in bonds. Add a pinch of an element with one extra electron per atom (a donor impurity) and you get N-type silicon, with free electrons. Add an element with one electron too few (an acceptor) and you get P-type, full of holes: gaps where an electron is missing, which behave like positive charges that move. Put the two side by side:

Near the boundary, electrons fall into holes and both disappear, leaving a thin depletion region with no free carriers at all: an insulating layer. Push from the P side (forward bias) and the layer thins until, at about 0.6–0.7 V for silicon, current flows freely. Push the other way (reverse bias) and the holes and electrons are pulled apart, the depletion region widens, and nothing flows. One direction only: that's a diode.

The P side is the anode; the N side is the cathode, marked by a stripe on the package. In the symbol, the arrow points the way conventional current flows, into the bar of the cathode.

The diode family

Every diode needs a little forward voltage before it conducts, and how much depends on what it's made of. Pick a type:

  • Silicon junction: about 0.7 V forward drop. The everyday rectifier.
  • Germanium: about 0.3 V. Old-fashioned, but good for detecting weak signals.
  • Schottky: a metal-to-semiconductor junction with a lower forward drop, so it wastes less power as a rectifier, and it's fast enough to be a VHF/UHF mixer or detector. A point-contact diode (a fine wire on a crystal, the "cat's whisker") is an RF detector too.
  • LED (light-emitting diode): forward current makes it glow. Its forward drop, around 2 V for red and 3 V for blue, is set by the material's band gap, which also sets the color. Used as indicators everywhere.
  • Zener: built to break down in reverse at a precise voltage, then hold that voltage constant however the current varies. A voltage reference, and a guard: Zeners between a MOSFET's gate and source protect it from static.
  • Varactor: the depletion region is an insulating gap between two conducting sides, so a reverse-biased diode is a capacitor whose value depends on the voltage: a voltage-controlled capacitor, used for electronic tuning.
  • PIN diode: an undoped layer in the middle gives it a very low junction capacitance, making it a good RF switch; its forward DC current controls how much RF it lets through (an RF attenuator).

Diodes fail from too much current because the junction gets too hot.

From AC to DC: rectifiers and filters

A radio runs on steady DC, but the wall gives AC. A power supply converts it in steps: a transformer sets the voltage, diodes (a rectifier) let only one direction through, a capacitor smooths the bumps, and a regulator holds the result steady. Try each:

  • A half-wave rectifier needs only one diode, and passes only half of each cycle (180°). The other half is simply lost.
  • A full-wave rectifier flips the negative half over, using all 360°. With a center-tapped transformer it takes two diodes; a bridge uses four. Unfiltered, its output is a series of DC pulses at twice the AC frequency.
  • The filter: capacitors (and sometimes inductors) store energy at each peak and release it in between, leaving only a small ripple. Full-wave ripple is easier to smooth, because the gaps are half as long.
  • Filter capacitors can hold a dangerous charge after the power is off. A bleeder resistor across them discharges them. When capacitors are stacked in series for high voltage, equal resistors across each one also share the voltage evenly. A step-start circuit lets big filter capacitors charge gradually at switch-on instead of with a huge surge.

Holding the voltage steady: regulators

Even after filtering, the voltage sags under load and rises with the mains. A regulator fixes that, in one of two ways:

  • A linear regulator is a variable resistor that adjusts itself: a transistor (the control element) in series with the load, conducting more or less to keep the output constant, compared against a stable reference (usually a Zener diode). The voltage it "drops" times the current is wasted as heat: (Vin − Vout) × I. It needs a minimum difference between input and output to work, its dropout voltage. The common three-terminal regulator chips are series regulators; a shunt regulator instead draws extra current from the source in parallel with the load.
  • A switchmode regulator switches fully on and fully off very fast, and a filter averages the pulses. It controls the output by varying the duty cycle (the fraction of time it's on). A switch that's fully on or fully off wastes almost nothing, and the high switching frequency allows tiny transformers and capacitors, which is why switching supplies are lighter and cheaper. The catch for radio: those fast edges make RF noise.

Batteries and the sun: battery operating time is its capacity in amp-hours divided by the average current (a 20 Ah battery at 4 A lasts about 5 hours). A solar panel makes DC; an inverter converts it to AC.

Transistors: valves a small current controls

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a sandwich of three semiconductor regions: the emitter, the base and the collector. A small current into the base lets a much larger current flow from collector to emitter, like a small hand on a big valve:

  • The ratio of collector current change to base current change is the transistor's beta: here 100. That multiplication is gain: a weak signal on the base becomes a strong copy at the collector. Gain means the output is bigger than the input, in voltage, current or power; transistors provide power gain.
  • A silicon NPN transistor starts to conduct when its base is about 0.6–0.7 V above its emitter (it's a diode junction).
  • As a switch, a transistor is driven between two extremes: cutoff (fully off) and saturation (fully on). In between is the active region, used for amplifying.

A field-effect transistor (FET) has a gate, drain and source. Instead of a base current, a voltage on the gate controls the current through a channel. In a MOSFET, the gate is separated from the channel by a thin insulating layer, so the gate draws practically no current: an FET has a much higher input impedance than a bipolar transistor. (That thin insulation is also why static electricity can destroy a MOSFET.) A depletion-mode FET conducts with no gate voltage at all; you have to apply voltage to turn it off. Before transistors there were vacuum tubes, where a control grid between the cathode and the plate does the controlling, and a screen grid reduces the capacitance between grid and plate.

Chips, packages and connectors

  • An integrated circuit (IC) combines many transistors and other parts in one package. An operational amplifier is an analog IC; CMOS digital logic uses much less power than older TTL.
  • MMICs (monolithic microwave integrated circuits) are amplifier blocks for VHF to microwaves: controlled gain, low noise, and 50 Ω in and out. They're connected with microstrip (a printed track over a ground plane) and powered through a resistor and/or RF choke on the output lead. A good UHF preamplifier has a noise figure around 0.5 dB.
  • For the highest frequencies: gallium arsenide (higher electron mobility, microwave circuits) and gallium nitride (highest frequency of the lot).
  • Packages: a DIP (dual in-line package) has two rows of pins for through-hole mounting; its long leads are too inductive for UHF. Surface-mount parts have the least parasitic effects at high frequencies.
  • Connectors: BNC (bayonet, good to about 4 GHz), N (moisture-resistant, to 10 GHz), SMA (small, threaded, several GHz), RCA phono (audio and DC). Ferrite cores come in different material "mixes" for different frequencies, and a ferrite bead on coax chokes off common-mode current by adding impedance in its path.

Light in, electricity out

  • A photovoltaic cell (solar cell) converts light to electricity: electrons absorb the light's energy. A silicon cell gives about 0.5 V in full sun; its efficiency is the fraction of light converted to current.
  • A photoconductive material's resistance drops when light shines on it.
  • An optoisolator is an LED shining on a phototransistor in one package: a signal crosses as light, with no electrical connection, which keeps a control circuit safely isolated from, say, 120 V AC. A solid-state relay is the same idea doing a mechanical relay's job. An optical shaft encoder detects rotation by interrupting a light beam with a patterned wheel (that's how many tuning knobs work).

Exam questions, explained

T6B02: What lets current flow in only one direction? A diode.

The PN junction: forward bias thins the depletion region; reverse bias widens it.

G7A07: Output of an unfiltered full-wave rectifier? DC pulses at twice the AC frequency.

Both halves of each cycle become a bump in the same direction: two bumps per cycle.

E6A06: What is beta? The change in collector current divided by the change in base current.

In the transistor scene, beta = 100: 1 mA more into the base, 100 mA more through the collector (until saturation).

E7D13: Power dissipated by a series linear regulator? Input-to-output voltage difference times output current.

The regulator is a self-adjusting resistor in series with the load: all the current goes through it, with the dropped voltage across it.

E6B04: A voltage-controlled capacitor? A varactor diode.

The depletion region is an insulating gap whose width depends on the reverse voltage.

Check yourself

Why does a silicon diode need about 0.7 V before it conducts?

That's the push needed to collapse its depletion region, the insulating layer at the junction. The exact value depends on the material.

A linear regulator turns 18 V into 13.8 V at 10 A. How much heat does it make?

(18 − 13.8) × 10 = 42 W. A switchmode regulator would waste far less.

Why is a MOSFET's input impedance so high?

Its gate is insulated from the channel by a thin oxide layer, so (at DC) no current flows into it at all.
