Capacitors and time

In chapter 1 everything happened instantly: change a resistor and the current changes right away. Add a capacitor and circuits start to have memory and time. That's how a camera flash stores a jolt, how a button press gets cleaned up, and how the radio course's filters keep one station and drop the rest.

In plain words: a capacitor is two metal plates with a thin gap. It can't let current straight through, but it can fill up with charge like a bucket. Empty, it gulps current; as it fills, its voltage rises and pushes back, so the current slows to a trickle. Its size, in farads, is how big the bucket is. Real ones are tiny: microfarads (µF), millionths of a farad.

1. Filling the bucket

A battery charges a capacitor through a resistor. Press Charge and watch in real time; then Discharge to let it empty through the same resistor. The graph shows the capacitor's voltage.

The curve always has the same shape, just stretched. Multiply R by C and you get the time constant, τ ("tau"), in seconds: after one τ the capacitor is 63% full, after five it's practically full. A bigger bucket or a narrower pipe both make it slower. That one number, R × C, is the clock of every circuit in this chapter.

🎯 Challenge: make the capacitor reach 63% in exactly 1 second (τ = 1 s, within 5%).

2. Running on stored charge

The battery fills a big capacitor, which also feeds an LED through a resistor. Now unplug the battery: the LED keeps glowing on the stored charge, then fades as the bucket empties.

🎯 Challenge: keep the LED visibly lit (above 1 mA) for at least 3 seconds after unplugging.

This is exactly what keeps an Arduino from resetting when a motor briefly pulls the supply down, and why a TV's power light fades after you unplug it.

3. The simplest filter

Now feed the resistor and capacitor a wave instead of a steady battery. A slow wave gives the capacitor time to fill and empty, so the output follows the input. A fast wave flips before the bucket can respond, so the output barely moves. That's a low-pass filter: low frequencies pass, high ones are squashed.

The dividing line is the cutoff frequency, fc = 1 / (2π·R·C): there the output is about 71% of the input. Each ten times higher in frequency, it drops ten times further. Try a square wave too: its sharp corners are made of high frequencies, so the filter rounds them off into curves. In the radio course this same idea, done with numbers instead of parts, is how one station gets picked out of the air (Radio ch. 6: averaging is filtering), and an FM radio's "75 µs de-emphasis" is exactly this circuit with R × C = 75 µs (Radio ch. 9).

🎯 Challenge: build a filter that lets a 100 Hz hum through (at least 90%) but cuts a 10 kHz whine below 10%.
