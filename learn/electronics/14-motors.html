Motors

Chapter 3's coil made a magnetic field from a current. Put that coil next to a magnet and the field pushes: that's a motor. This chapter covers the three kinds you'll actually use: a plain DC motor (fast, cheap, needs an H-bridge to reverse), a hobby servo (goes to an angle you name with a pulse), and a stepper (moves exactly one notch per pulse), along with the electronics that drive each.

In plain words: current through a wire in a magnetic field gets a sideways push. Coil the wire around a shaft between magnets and the push becomes turning. A microcontroller pin can't supply the amps a motor needs, so a transistor (or four) does the heavy lifting while the pin only says when.

1. A DC motor, and the voltage it makes back

Torque is proportional to current: \(\tau = k\,I\). But a spinning motor is also a generator (chapter 3: a moving magnet in a coil induces a voltage), and that back-EMF \(k\,\omega\) opposes the supply. So the current is set by what's left over: \(I = (V - k\omega)/R\). A free-running motor speeds up until back-EMF almost cancels the supply and only a small current flows. Load it, it slows, back-EMF drops, more current flows, more torque: it balances itself. Hold the shaft still (stall) and nothing opposes the supply: the current is just \(V/R\), the largest it can be.

\[ I = \frac{V - k\,\omega}{R}, \qquad \tau = k\,I, \qquad I_\text{stall} = \frac{V}{R} \]

🎯 Challenge: stall the motor (drag the load until it stops) and read the stall current.

A 6 V motor has a winding resistance of 2 Ω. What's its stall current, in amps?

\(6 / 2 = 3\) A, maybe ten times its running current. That's what the driver, the battery and the wires must survive at every start (a motor starts from standstill, so it starts at stall current) and why a stuck motor overheats: all that current goes into heating the winding.

2. The H-bridge

To run a motor both ways you need to swap which terminal is positive. Four switches (MOSFETs, chapter 4) in an "H" do it: close the top-left and bottom-right and current flows left to right through the motor; close the other diagonal and it flows right to left. Close both bottoms and the motor's terminals are shorted together: its own back-EMF drives a current that brakes it hard. Close two switches on the same side and you've connected the supply straight to ground: shoot-through, which in real life means smoke. Driver chips (L298, DRV8833, TB6612) contain the four switches plus the logic that makes shoot-through impossible.

🎯 Challenge: run the motor forward, then reverse it, then brake it.

When a running motor's H-bridge switches off, the motor's coil still carries current and wants to keep it flowing (chapter 3). Where does it go?

Through diodes across each switch (MOSFETs have one built in, the "body diode"), back to the supply. Without them the coil's voltage spikes until something breaks down: the same flyback problem as chapter 4's single MOSFET driving a relay, four times over.

3. Speed control with PWM

Chapter 13's PWM drives one switch of the bridge: at 50% duty the motor sees, on average, half the supply. The coil's inductance smooths the current between pulses (it can't change quickly), so the motor spins steadily rather than jerking. Pick the PWM frequency above 20 kHz and you can't hear it whine. Below, the duty sets the average voltage that section 1's motor runs on.

4. Hobby servos: an angle from a pulse width

A servo is a small DC motor, a gearbox, a potentiometer on the output shaft (chapter 11's sensor) and a control circuit in one box. You send it a pulse every 20 ms (50 Hz), and the pulse's width is the angle you want: 1 ms means one end, 2 ms the other, 1.5 ms the middle. The circuit compares the pot's reading with the pulse and drives the motor until they match: a closed loop, like chapter 7's op-amp chasing its two inputs to equal.

A servo maps 1.0 ms → 0° and 2.0 ms → 180°, linearly. What angle does a 1.75 ms pulse give, in degrees?

0.75 of the way from 1 ms to 2 ms, so \(0.75 \times 180 = 135°\). (Real servos vary; many go from about 0.5 to 2.5 ms, so calibrate the ends.)

5. Steppers: one notch per pulse

A stepper has two coils and a magnetized rotor with many teeth. Energize coil A one way, and the rotor snaps to the nearest position that lines up with A. Then B, then A reversed, then B reversed: each step moves the rotor one notch, 1.8° for the common kind, 200 steps per turn. No sensor needed: count the steps and you know where it is (as long as it never skips). That's why 3D printers and plotters use them. Energize both coils at once between full steps and the rotor sits halfway: half-stepping, twice the resolution.

How many full steps turn a 1.8° stepper by exactly 90°?

\(90 / 1.8 = 50\). Microstepping drivers (A4988, TMC2209) go further, feeding the coils sine-shaped currents to sit anywhere between notches, 16 or more positions per step, and much quieter.

A robot arm's base must hold any angle precisely, all day, with no position sensor. A wheel just has to go fast. A camera pan needs an angle, cheaply. Which motor goes where?

  • DC on the arm, stepper on the wheel, servo on the camera
  • Servo on the arm, stepper on the wheel, DC on the camera
  • Stepper on the arm, DC on the wheel, servo on the camera
The stepper counts its way to positions, the DC motor is the cheap fast spinner, and the servo is an angle for one wire and a pulse. (Big robot arms actually use DC or brushless motors with encoders, which is the servo idea scaled up.)
