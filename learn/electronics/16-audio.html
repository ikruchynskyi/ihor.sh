Audio

Audio is the whole course in one signal chain. A microphone turns air pressure into a few millivolts, an amplifier (chapter 7's op-amps, then transistors that can push current) makes it big, and a speaker (chapter 14's motor, moving in a line instead of a circle) turns it back into air. Along the way it's chapter 8's filters, chapter 10's power supplies, chapter 13's PWM, and the ear as the final judge: you'll hear each amplifier's mistakes below.

In plain words: sound is the air pressure wiggling, 20 to 20,000 times a second. A microphone makes a voltage that wiggles the same way, an amplifier makes a bigger copy of that voltage, and a speaker uses the copy to push the air. Everything that goes wrong is the copy not being exact.

1. Microphones: pressure to voltage

A dynamic microphone is a speaker run backwards: a diaphragm moves a coil in a magnet's field and induces a voltage (chapter 3), no power needed, about 2 mV for speech. An electret capsule is a capacitor (chapter 2) whose one plate is the diaphragm, permanently charged; sound changes the gap and so the voltage, and a tiny built-in transistor needs a few volts of bias through a resistor to read it out. A MEMS microphone (every phone and laptop) is the same idea etched in silicon with its own ADC, sending numbers over I²S (chapter 13's buses again). Here's yours, if you let the page listen:

2. Decibels: from millivolts to watts

Audio levels span a huge range, so they're counted in decibels: every ×10 in voltage is +20 dB, every ×2 is about +6 dB. A dynamic mic gives ~2 mV, "line level" between devices is ~1 V, and a speaker at a loud party gets ~10 V across it. Each stage of a chain has a gain in dB, and the gains just add.

\[ G_\text{dB} = 20 \log_{10} \frac{V_\text{out}}{V_\text{in}} \]

A preamp turns a 10 mV microphone signal into 1 V. What's its gain in dB?

\(1 / 0.01 = 100\), and \(20 \log_{10} 100 = 40\) dB. Two ×10 steps, 20 dB each.

3. Amplifier classes, drawn and played

The output stage has to push real current into a speaker, and the ways of doing it trade quality against heat:

  • Class A: one transistor biased half-on all the time, so it amplifies the whole wave smoothly. It also burns power even in silence: at most 25% of the supply's power reaches the speaker (with a simple resistor load).
  • Class B: two transistors in push-pull, one for the positive half, one for the negative. Nothing flows at rest, up to 78.5% efficient. But each transistor needs ~0.6 V to turn on (chapter 4), so near zero neither conducts: a little flat step at every zero crossing, crossover distortion, that sounds harsh exactly when the music is quiet.
  • Class AB: class B with a small idle current through both so the handover is seamless. Almost every hi-fi amplifier.
  • Class D: the output transistors only switch fully on or off at hundreds of kHz (chapter 13's PWM, with the duty following the music), and an LC filter (chapter 8) smooths it back to audio. Switches waste almost nothing: 90%+, which is why a tiny Bluetooth speaker runs all day.

Why is crossover distortion worse at low volume, while clipping is worse at high volume?

  • The transistors heat up when quiet
  • The dead zone is a fixed ±0.6 V: a big wave barely notices it, a small wave is mostly dead zone. Clipping only starts when the wave reaches the supply rails.
  • The speaker distorts quiet sounds
Distortion is the share of the output that isn't the input. A fixed-size flaw is a big share of a small wave; the rails are a limit only big waves meet. That's why THD (total harmonic distortion) specs always say "at 1 W" or similar.

4. Speakers: power into ohms

A speaker is a coil glued to a paper or plastic cone, sitting in a magnet's gap: the current pushes the coil in and out (chapter 14's force, \(F = B I \ell\)). Its impedance, usually 4 or 8 Ω, is mostly the coil's resistance (plus its inductance, which rises with frequency). Power is chapter 1's \(P = V^2/R\), with the RMS voltage (a sine's peak ÷ √2).

\[ P = \frac{V_\text{rms}^2}{R}, \qquad V_\text{rms} = \frac{V_\text{peak}}{\sqrt{2}} \]

What RMS voltage drives an 8 Ω speaker at 10 W?

\(V = \sqrt{P R} = \sqrt{80} \approx 8.94\) V RMS, a 12.6 V peak, so the amplifier's supply has to be at least ±13 V (or 26 V for a bridged class D). Doubling the loudness you hear takes about ten times the power.

A 4 Ω speaker on the same amplifier, at the same voltage, takes twice the power of an 8 Ω one. Why can that be a problem?

Twice the power means twice the current through the output transistors (\(I = V/R\)), and their heat grows with current. An amplifier rated for 8 Ω may overheat, or shut down, on 4 Ω. That's why speakers and amplifiers state their impedance.

That's the electronics track. To build any of it, start in the circuit lab (op-amp amplifiers and filters are ready-made circuits), and the radio course is where filters, mixing and audio meet again, in software.
