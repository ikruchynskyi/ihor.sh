AI from scratch · Chapter 9

Vision in practice

Chapter 6 taught a network to read digits on a black square. Real images are messier: rain, glare, night, a bus blocking half the view. NYC publishes about 900 live traffic cameras, refreshed every couple of seconds. Let's point everything we've built at them: first plain arithmetic, then a real trained detector running in your browser.

1. A live camera

A frame is about 350 × 240 pixels, each with red, green and blue values: some 250,000 numbers. That's what every method below starts from.

2. Motion, by subtraction

The simplest vision trick needs no learning at all. Take two frames a few seconds apart and subtract them, pixel by pixel. The road, buildings and sky cancel out; whatever moved lights up. Traffic engineers used this long before neural networks.

It finds motion but not what moved: two cars side by side merge into one blob, a parked car is invisible, and swaying trees or a camera adjusting its exposure light up as false alarms. To know what's there, we need a model that has learned what cars look like.

3. A trained detector

This is COCO-SSD: a convolutional network (chapter 6, but dozens of layers deep, based on MobileNet) trained on the COCO dataset of 330,000 photos labeled with 80 kinds of objects. For each image it proposes boxes and gives each one a class and a confidence: a softmax (chapter 5) per box. Loading it downloads about 5 MB once, and then it runs entirely in your browser; no image leaves your computer.

Slide the confidence down and more boxes appear, including wrong ones; slide it up and real cars far away disappear. Every detector lives on that trade-off between missing things and imagining them. Detectors also produce many overlapping boxes for each object; a step called non-maximum suppression keeps only the most confident box among ones that overlap a lot.

4. Counting the city, right now

Put the detector on a loop over many cameras and you get something no single camera gives you: a snapshot of the city. It counts objects only; at this resolution nobody can be identified, and nothing is stored.

This is the pattern behind real traffic-counting systems: a fixed, pretrained model plus your own aggregation and archive. The "AI" part is a component; the value is in what you build around it, here a count across a city at one instant, which you could log every five minutes and turn into a busyness map. That's where this world ends: from matrices as moves of the plane, through gradients, neurons, backprop, probability, convolutions, attention and agents, to a model reading the streets of New York.
