AI from scratch · Chapter 5

Probability and loss

So far our networks said "blue or red". Real ones choose among many answers: which digit, which animal, which of 100,000 words comes next. They don't output an answer; they output how much they believe in each one. This chapter is about those beliefs: how scores become probabilities, how to measure a wrong belief, and why a chatbot's reply can change every time you ask.

1. Scores become probabilities: softmax

The last layer of a network gives one raw score per answer, called a logit: any number, positive or negative. To turn scores into probabilities that are positive and add up to 1, we use softmax: raise e to each score, then divide by the total.

p(i) = escore(i) / T / Σ escore(j) / T

Drag the scores. Only the differences between them matter: add the same amount to all three and nothing changes. A score 2 higher means about 7 times more likely (e² ≈ 7.4). The temperature divides every score first: low temperature exaggerates the differences until the top answer takes everything; high temperature flattens them toward a coin flip.

2. Loss as surprise: cross-entropy

To train, we need a number for "how wrong was that belief?". The standard answer: look only at the probability the model gave to the right answer, p, and take −log p. That's the cross-entropy loss, also called surprise: 100% sure and right means zero surprise; 1% on the truth means a lot.

The curve shoots up near zero. That's on purpose: a model that's confidently wrong gets a huge loss, so its gradient is big and it changes a lot, while a model that's unsure gets a gentle push. It's the same log loss our single neuron used in chapter 3, now for any number of answers.

3. Why a model "guesses": sampling

A language model is this exact machine with one answer per word in its vocabulary. Given "The cat sat on the", it gives every word a probability. Then it has to pick one. Always picking the top word is called greedy; it's predictable and tends to repeat itself. Instead, models usually sample: roll a weighted die. Try it, and try the temperature.

The cat sat on the …

That's why the same question can get different answers, and why "temperature" is a setting in every chatbot API: near 0 it's careful and repetitive, higher it's creative and occasionally unhinged. The model doesn't "know" the next word; it knows a distribution, and the reply is one roll of the dice.

4. The gradient is "what you said minus what was true"

Here's the beautiful part. Put softmax and cross-entropy together and do the backprop from chapter 4, and the gradient for each score comes out as simply

∂loss / ∂score(i) = p(i) − (1 if i is the right answer, else 0)

The right answer's score gets pushed up by how much probability it was missing; every wrong answer gets pushed down by how much it took. Pick the right answer and train.

Every classifier and every language model is trained with exactly this push, on billions of examples. Next we'll give networks eyes: convolutions, filters that slide over images.
