Taylor series and curvature

The derivative says which way is downhill. It doesn't say how far you can go before downhill turns into uphill. That's the second derivative's job: curvature. This lesson builds functions out of polynomials, uses that to explain exactly when gradient descent works and when a learning rate makes it explode, and shows why stretched valleys make training zigzag and why the obvious fix, Newton's method, is out of reach for neural networks.

1. Any smooth function is a polynomial, up close

Calculus 1 replaced a curve by its tangent line near a point \(c\). Why stop at a line? Match the value, the slope, the curvature, and so on, one derivative per term:

\[ f(x) \approx f(c) + f'(c)(x - c) + \frac{f''(c)}{2!}(x - c)^2 + \frac{f'''(c)}{3!}(x - c)^3 + \dots \]

f its Taylor polynomial

Order 0 is a flat line, order 1 the tangent, order 2 a parabola that also bends the right way. Each new term hugs the curve over a wider stretch. For \(e^x\) and \(\sin x\) adding terms eventually works everywhere; for \(\ln(1+x)\) around 0 it only helps for \(x\) between −1 and 1, and beyond that more terms make it worse. Try it.

Using the order-2 polynomial of \(e^x\) at \(c = 0\), that is \(1 + x + x^2/2\), estimate \(e^{0.1}\).

\(1 + 0.1 + 0.005 = 1.105\). The true value is 1.10517: the next term, \(0.1^3/6 \approx 0.00017\), is all that's missing. Near the center, each term is much smaller than the last.

ML lives almost entirely on the first two orders. The loss near the current weights is a slope plus a curvature: \(L(w + \Delta) \approx L(w) + L'(w)\,\Delta + \tfrac12 L''(w)\,\Delta^2\). The next sections are this one line put to work.

2. Why gradient descent works, and when it blows up

Gradient descent steps \(\Delta = -\eta L'\). Put that into the second-order picture:

\[ L(w - \eta L') \approx L - \eta L'^2 + \tfrac12 L'' \eta^2 L'^2 = L - \eta L'^2 \left(1 - \tfrac12 \eta L''\right) \]

For small \(\eta\) the loss drops: that's the guarantee. But the bracket turns negative once \(\eta > 2/L''\): the step overshoots so far that the far side of the valley is higher than where you started. The cleanest case is the parabola \(L = \tfrac12 k w^2\), with slope \(kw\) and curvature \(k\) everywhere. One step does \(w \leftarrow w - \eta k w = (1 - \eta k)\,w\). Each step multiplies the distance to the bottom by \(1 - \eta k\):

Below \(\eta = 1/k\) the ball creeps down one side. At exactly \(1/k\) it lands on the bottom in one step. Between \(1/k\) and \(2/k\) it overshoots and bounces, but each bounce is smaller. Past \(2/k\) every bounce is bigger: the loss goes to infinity, the "loss is NaN" you'll see in real training. A real network has different curvatures in different directions, and the steepest one sets the limit for all of them.

The curvature is \(k = 4\). Below what learning rate does gradient descent converge?

\(|1 - 4\eta| < 1\) means \(0 < \eta < 0.5\), that is \(2/k\). Set k = 4 above and slide η across 0.5.

3. Stretched valleys: the Hessian and the condition number

With many weights, the curvature becomes a matrix of second partials, the Hessian \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial w_i \partial w_j\) (it's the Jacobian of the gradient, from Calculus 2). Take the simplest valley, \(L = \tfrac12(b x^2 + a y^2)\): the Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} b & 0 \\ 0 & a \end{pmatrix}\), gentle along the valley (\(b = 1\), in \(x\)) and steep across it (\(a\), in \(y\)). The ratio \(\kappa = a/b\), the largest curvature over the smallest, is the condition number.

gradient descent Newton

Section 2's rule now applies per direction: η must stay under \(2/a\) for the steep direction, so the gentle direction shrinks by only \(1 - \eta b\) per step, under \(1 - 2/\kappa\). The steps needed grow in proportion to \(\kappa\). Gradient descent zigzags across the valley while barely moving along it. Real networks have condition numbers in the thousands and up, which is why plain gradient descent is slow and why optimizers like momentum and Adam exist (Part 3).

Newton's method fixes it outright: step by \(-H^{-1}\nabla L\) instead of \(-\eta \nabla L\). Dividing by the curvature makes every direction equally easy; on a quadratic it lands on the minimum in one step, whatever \(\kappa\) is. Tick the box.

So why don't we train networks with Newton's method? A model has \(10^9\) parameters. How many entries does its Hessian have?

\(10^9 \times 10^9 = 10^{18}\): eight exabytes in 64-bit floats, before you even try to invert it (about \(n^3 = 10^{27}\) operations). Optimizers settle for cheap hints of curvature instead, like Adam's per-weight running average of squared gradients.

Away from a toy, Newton's method shows its real strength: near a minimum, the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. Here it is against gradient descent on \(f(x) = e^x - 2x\), whose minimum is at \(x = \ln 2\) (where \(f' = e^x - 2 = 0\)):

Gradient descent's error shrinks by a fixed factor each step. Newton's error is roughly squared each step: 0.04, 0.0009, \(4 \times 10^{-7}\), \(8 \times 10^{-14}\), then exact to the last digit.

4. Minimum, maximum or saddle

Where the gradient is zero, the Hessian's eigenvalues (the curvatures along its principal directions, chapter 1's "stretch factors") decide what you're standing on:

eigenvaluespointpicture
all positiveminimuma bowl: up in every direction
all negativemaximuma hilltop
mixed signssaddlea mountain pass: up along the ridge, down across it

At a point where the gradient is zero, the Hessian has eigenvalues 3 and −2. What is it?

  • A minimum
  • A maximum
  • A saddle
Curving up one way (3) and down the other (−2) is a saddle, like \(1.5x^2 - y^2\).

In a million dimensions, a minimum needs a million curvatures to all come out positive. If signs were coin flips, that's \(2^{-1{,}000{,}000}\). 5. What to carry forward

  • Taylor: near a point, a smooth function is its value + slope × Δ + ½ curvature × Δ² + …; ML mostly uses the first two orders.
  • Learning rate limit: gradient descent is stable only for \(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\), where \(\lambda_{\max}\) is the steepest curvature. Past it, the loss explodes.
  • Condition number \(\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\): the bigger it is, the more gradient descent zigzags, and the steps needed grow like \(\kappa\).
  • Newton divides by curvature and converges fast, but a Hessian with \(n^2\) entries is out of reach for big models; practical optimizers approximate it.
  • Saddles, not local minima, are the typical flat spots in high dimensions.
