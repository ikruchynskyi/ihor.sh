Math warm-up

Machine learning papers look scary mostly because of their notation. Underneath, the first few stages use school math: functions, powers, logarithms and adding things up. This lesson goes over exactly those, each with a picture you drag and a box you run, and ends with you reading real ML formulas out loud like sentences. If something here already feels easy, skim it and do the questions.

1. Functions and their graphs

A function takes a number in and gives a number out. \(f(x) = x^2\) says "call the input \(x\), the output is \(x\) times \(x\)": so \(f(3) = 9\). The graph of a function is every pair (input, output) drawn as a dot: across is the input, up is the output. Pick a function and drag \(x\): the yellow dot is \((x, f(x))\).

You'll meet all five again. The line is the taxi model from Start 1. The bowl is what an error looks like as you turn a knob: one lowest point, and training rolls into it. \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) are the next two sections. The S-curve, called the sigmoid, squeezes any number into the range 0 to 1, so a model can turn a score into a probability (chapter 3 builds a neuron with it).

If \(f(x) = x^2 - 2x\), what is \(f(3)\)?

Put 3 in for every \(x\): \(3^2 - 2 \times 3 = 9 - 6 = 3\).

Pick the sigmoid and drag \(x\) far to the right, then far to the left. What happens to the output?

  • It keeps growing without limit
  • It gets close to 1 on the right and close to 0 on the left, but never reaches either
  • It's 0.5 everywhere
That's exactly why it's used for "probability of yes": any score, however big or negative, lands strictly between 0 and 1, and a score of 0 means 50/50.

Many functions take several inputs: \(f(a, b) = a \times 4 + b\) is the taxi model's guess for a 4 km ride as you turn both knobs. Stage 1 draws functions of two inputs as landscapes you walk over.

2. Powers, and very big and very small numbers

\(2^5\) means five 2s multiplied: \(2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 32\). Three rules follow just from counting how many copies get multiplied:

ruleexamplewhy
\(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\)\(2^3 \times 2^4 = 2^7 = 128\)3 copies, then 4 more
\(a^{-n} = 1 / a^n\)\(2^{-3} = 1/8 = 0.125\)each step down the powers divides by 2: 8, 4, 2, 1, ½, ¼, ⅛
\(a^{1/2} = \sqrt{a}\)\(9^{1/2} = 3\)\(9^{1/2} \times 9^{1/2} = 9^1\), so it's the number that squares to 9

ML numbers come in wildly different sizes: a learning rate of 0.001, a model with 7,000,000,000 weights. Scientific notation writes them as a number times a power of 10: \(0.001 = 10^{-3}\) and \(7{,}000{,}000{,}000 = 7 \times 10^9\). Code writes the same thing as 1e-3 and 7e9: "e" here means "times ten to the", nothing to do with the number \(e\).

What is \(2^{-3}\) as a decimal?

\(2^{-3} = 1 / 2^3 = 1/8 = 0.125\).

A config file says lr = 3e-4. Write that learning rate as a plain decimal.

\(3 \times 10^{-4} = 3 \times 0.0001 = 0.0003\). 3e-4 is a famously common learning rate for the Adam optimizer (stage 4).

The number \(e \approx 2.718\) is special because \(e^x\) grows at a rate equal to its own value (Calculus 1 shows why that makes it the easy one). You'll mostly see it in \(e^x\), written Math.exp(x) in code.

3. Logarithms: counting the multiplications

A logarithm answers "what power?". \(\log_2 8 = 3\) because \(2^3 = 8\): it counts how many 2s you multiply to get 8. \(\log_{10} 1000 = 3\) counts the zeros. And the natural log \(\ln x\) is the power of \(e\): \(\ln x = t\) means \(e^t = x\). So \(\ln\) undoes \(e^x\), and the two graphs are mirror images across the dashed line \(y = x\). Drag \(t\):

The one rule that matters most in ML: a log turns multiplying into adding, \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). (It follows from the first power rule: multiplying powers of \(e\) adds the exponents.) A model's probability of a whole dataset is the product of its probabilities for each example: thousands of numbers below 1 multiplied together. That underflows to exactly 0 on a computer, so models work with the sum of the logs instead. Run it:

What is \(\log_2 1024\)? (How many times do you double 1 to reach 1024?)

1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024: ten doublings, so 10. Logs turn huge ranges into small, even steps, which is why plots of training loss often use a log scale.

Three examples each get probability 0.5. What is \(\ln(0.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.5)\)? (\(\ln 0.5 \approx -0.693\).)

\(\ln 0.5 + \ln 0.5 + \ln 0.5 = 3 \times (-0.693) = -2.079\). Logs of probabilities are always negative or zero (since probabilities are at most 1), which is why the loss in chapter 5, cross-entropy, puts a minus sign in front.

What is \(\ln 1\)?

  • 1
  • It doesn't exist
  • 0
\(e^0 = 1\), so \(\ln 1 = 0\). A model that gives the right answer probability 1 has a log-probability of 0: no loss at all.

4. The Σ sign is a loop

The big Greek S, \(\Sigma\) (sigma), means "add these up". The bits around it say what to add:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{4} i^2 = 1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 = 30 \]

Read it as: "for \(i\) from 1 to 4, add up \(i^2\)". Underneath is where the counter starts, on top is where it stops, and to the right is what you add each time. It's exactly the loop from Start 2. Change the sum below:

Datasets are written with a counter too: \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\) are the \(n\) examples, and \(x_i\) is "the \(i\)-th one". With that, the mean is

\[ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \]

"x-bar is one over n times the sum of all the x's": add them up, divide by how many. (The 1/n out front does the dividing.) And a product of many terms uses the big Greek P, \(\prod\), the same way.

What is \(\sum_{i=1}^{4} 2i\)?

\(2 + 4 + 6 + 8 = 20\). Or take the 2 outside: \(2 \times (1 + 2 + 3 + 4) = 2 \times 10\). Constants can always come out of a sum, a trick that tidies up a lot of derivations.

Set the sum to \(1/2^i\) and slide \(n\) up to 12. If you kept adding forever, what number would the sum get closer and closer to?

\(\frac12 + \frac14 + \frac18 + \dots\) heads to 1: each new term covers half the gap that's left. Infinitely many terms can add up to a finite number. Stage 4's momentum optimizer is built on a sum just like this one.

6. Reading a formula out loud

Every formula is a sentence. Read it left to right, say each symbol's name, and turn it into a recipe. Try each one before revealing.

Read this one out loud, as a recipe: \[ \hat{y} = \sigma(w x + b) \]

"y-hat equals sigma of w x plus b." Recipe: multiply the input by the weight, add the bias, push the result through the S-curve. The output is the model's guess, a number between 0 and 1. That is one neuron, the whole of chapter 3.

And this one: \[ L = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2 \]

"L equals one over n times the sum, for i from 1 to n, of y-hat i minus y i, squared." Recipe: for every example, take the guess minus the truth and square it; add those up; divide by how many. That's the mean squared error you computed in Start 2's last box.

And the most important line in all of ML: \[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \nabla L(\theta) \]

"theta becomes theta minus eta times the gradient of L at theta." Recipe: find which way each knob would raise the loss, step every knob a small amount (\(\eta\)) the other way, repeat. That's gradient descent, the "Let the computer train it" button from Start 1. Stage 1 is about computing \(\nabla L\).

Do one step of that by hand: the knob is \(\theta = 3\), the learning rate is \(\eta = 0.1\), and the gradient is \(\nabla L = 4\). What is \(\theta\) after the step?

\(3 - 0.1 \times 4 = 3 - 0.4 = 2.6\). The gradient is positive, meaning a bigger \(\theta\) gives a bigger loss, so the knob moves down.

7. What to carry forward

  • A function maps inputs to an output; its graph plots every (input, output) pair. A model is a function.
  • Powers multiply copies; negative powers divide; 3e-4 means \(3 \times 10^{-4}\).
  • A log asks "which power?", undoes \(e^x\), and turns products into sums, which is why models add log-probabilities instead of multiplying probabilities.
  • \(\Sigma\) is a loop that adds; \(\frac{1}{n}\Sigma\) is a mean.
  • Greek letters are just names for knobs and settings: \(\theta\) the knobs, \(\eta\) the step size, \(\hat{y}\) the guess, \(L\) the loss.
