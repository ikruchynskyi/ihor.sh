Support vector machines

Before deep learning, support vector machines were the most respected classifiers there were: elegant math, one global optimum, and state-of-the-art results on handwriting and text. They're less common now, but their two ideas live on everywhere: the margin (don't just separate the classes, separate them with room to spare) and the kernel trick (bend the boundary by measuring similarity, without ever building new columns).

1. The idea in one picture: the widest street

Many lines separate two clouds. An SVM picks the one in the middle of the widest street between them: the dashed lines are the street's edges, the solid one its middle. The circled dots are the support vectors: the dots on or inside the edges. Only they decide where the street goes; move any other dot (not across the street) and nothing changes.

Pick the bendy kernel and slide γ to the top. Predict first: what happens to the support vectors and to validation accuracy?

  • Almost every dot becomes a support vector, training accuracy nears 100%, validation drops
  • The boundary gets smoother
  • Nothing changes, it just trains slower
γ sets how far a dot's influence reaches. At large γ, each dot is only similar to itself, so the boundary becomes a bubble around each training dot (memorizing), and every dot is needed to describe it. Measured on handwritten digits: γ = 0.001 gets 99.1% with half the training rows as support vectors; γ = 0.01, 79.6% with 99.5% of them; γ = 0.1, 10%, chance.

An SVM finds the boundary with the widest margin, decided only by the points nearest to it; a kernel lets that boundary bend by measuring similarity instead of adding columns.

2. How it learns: hinge loss plus a price on narrow streets

With labels \(y_i = \pm 1\) and a score \(f(x) = w \cdot x + b\), the street's edges are where \(f = \pm 1\), so its width is \(2/\lVert w \rVert\): small weights mean a wide street. The SVM minimizes

\[ \tfrac12 \lVert w \rVert^2 \;+\; C \sum_i \max\big(0,\; 1 - y_i f(x_i)\big) \]

The second part is the hinge loss: zero for a dot safely on its side beyond the edge, growing linearly as it slips into the street or across. Compare page 2's log-loss, which never quite reaches zero: every dot pulls a little, however far away. With hinge loss, far dots pull not at all, which is exactly why only the support vectors matter. C trades the two: a small C accepts dots inside the street for a wider one (smoother, more support vectors); a large C narrows the street to put every dot on its side.

A dot sits on the correct side with \(y f(x) = 3\), far beyond the edge. How much does it change the fitted SVM if you delete it?

  • A little: every point counts
  • Not at all: its hinge loss is zero and it's not a support vector
  • A lot: it's the most confident point
\(\max(0, 1 - 3) = 0\): it contributes nothing to the loss and nothing to the gradient. The solution is the same with or without it. That sparsity is the SVM's signature, and on the bank calls it's a weakness too: the model ignores the bulk of easy cases and lives on the ambiguous ones.
Why the kernel trick works
  1. The optimum can always be written as a weighted sum of training points, \(w = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\), with \(\alpha_i = 0\) for every point that isn't a support vector. (Only margin points push on the solution.)
  2. So the score is \(f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \,(x_i \cdot x) + b\): the data enters only through dot products between points.
  3. Now map each point to new columns \(\phi(x)\), say all squares and products, and fit a line there: the score needs only \(\phi(x_i)\cdot\phi(x)\). For 2 columns, \((a \cdot b + 1)^2\) equals \(\phi(a)\cdot\phi(b)\) where \(\phi\) lists 6 columns (1, each column, each square, the product), each with the right constant in front: a dot product in 6 columns, computed from one in 2.
  4. A kernel \(K(a, b)\) is any such shortcut. The RBF kernel \(K(a, b) = e^{-\gamma \lVert a - b \rVert^2}\) is a dot product in infinitely many columns, computed from a distance.
  5. So the SVM fits a straight street in a space too big to build, and on the map it bends. The cost: the model is a sum over support vectors, and training compares every pair of points (section 4).

4. Where it shines, where it breaks

Shines: medium-sized data with many columns. On digits, the RBF SVM gets 98.7% with default settings and 99.1% tuned, the best of any model in the zoo. On the 7,166 word columns of the text messages, a linear SVM gets 97.9% in 4 milliseconds with no tuning. Its optimum is unique (the loss is convex, calculus 5), so there's no seed lottery.

Breaks: training compares pairs of points, so time grows roughly with the square of the rows. Measured on 10 columns: 2,000 rows 0.02 s, 4,000 rows 0.09 s, 8,000 rows 0.3 s, 16,000 rows 1.2 s. Each doubling multiplies the time by about 4, so a million rows would take hours where boosting takes seconds.

On the bank calls, the RBF SVM's AUC is 0.711, the lowest in the zoo (every other model reaches at least 0.74). Which reason fits best?

  • The columns weren't scaled
  • Too much data
  • Its scores come from distances to the hardest cases, on a table of one-hot categories and skewed counts where distance means little
An RBF kernel says "similar rows are near each other". For 63 one-hot and economic columns, nearness is a weak notion of similarity (the same problem kNN had on page 3), and the hinge loss throws away the easy cases that carry most of the calibration. Trees and linear models simply fit the table better. SVMs are for data where a distance means something: pixels, word counts, measurements on one scale.

SVMs shine on clean, medium-sized, many-column data where distance means something; they're slow on big data, sensitive to scaling and γ, and give scores rather than probabilities.

Where it breaks, and what people do
  • Big data. Kernel SVMs scale like rows squared or worse. For millions of rows use a linear SVM (LinearSVC, SGDClassifier(loss="hinge")), or approximate the kernel with random features (RBFSampler, Nystroem) and fit a linear model on them.
  • γ and C. They interact, and the good region can be narrow (digits: 99.1% at γ = 0.001, 10% at 0.1). Search both on a log grid with cross-validation.
  • Scaling. The RBF kernel is a distance, so unscaled columns hijack it, as on page 3 (bank calls: 0.677 unscaled, 0.711 scaled).
  • No probabilities. The score \(f(x)\) is a signed distance, not a chance. probability=True fits a sigmoid on cross-validated scores (Platt scaling), which costs five extra fits and can disagree with predict.
  • Tables. Mixed categories and counts: use boosted trees or logistic regression first.
