Singular value decomposition: rotate, stretch, rotate

Linear algebra 2 found the arrows a matrix only stretches, but only some matrices have enough of them, and only symmetric ones keep them at right angles. The singular value decomposition (SVD) works for every matrix, any shape: each one is a rotation, then a stretch along the axes, then another rotation. Ordering the stretches from biggest to smallest turns out to be one of the most useful ideas in machine learning: compression, PCA, recommendations, solving equations that have no exact answer, and fine-tuning big models cheaply all come from it.

1. Every matrix turns a circle into an ellipse

Drag the blue and yellow arrows: they're where the matrix sends the steps \(\hat\imath\) and \(\hat\jmath\) (chapter 1). The gray circle of unit arrows becomes a tilted ellipse, always. Its longest radius is the most the matrix stretches anything, its shortest the least. The green and pink arrows are the two unit arrows that land exactly on the ellipse's axes. Now press Play: the same matrix as three simple moves.

\[ A = U\,\Sigma\,V^\top \qquad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge 0 \]

Read it right to left, like a sequence of moves: \(V^\top\) rotates the plane so the two directions that will become the ellipse's axes (the columns of \(V\), the right singular vectors) lie along x and y; \(\Sigma\) stretches x by \(\sigma_1\) and y by \(\sigma_2\), the singular values; \(U\) rotates the result into place, its columns pointing along the ellipse's axes. (When the matrix flips the plane over, one stretch is shown negative.) In more dimensions it's the same: an \(m \times n\) matrix rotates, stretches along up to \(\min(m, n)\) axes, and rotates again.

A pure rotation by 30° (chapter 1's rotation(30)): what are its singular values? Give the larger one.

Rotations don't stretch anything: the circle stays a circle, so \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1\). That's also why its determinant is 1 (the area doesn't change).

2. Singular values and eigenvalues

For a symmetric matrix (a covariance, a Hessian), the two ideas meet: singular values are the eigenvalues' sizes, and \(U\) and \(V\) are the eigenvectors. For other matrices they differ, and the SVD still exists when eigenvectors don't (a shear has only one eigen-direction, but its circle still becomes an ellipse). The link that always holds: \(A^\top A\) is symmetric, its eigenvalues are \(\sigma_i^2\), and its eigenvectors are \(V\). The ratio \(\sigma_1/\sigma_2\) is linear algebra 1's condition number: how stretched the ellipse is, and how much rounding errors get magnified when you solve with this matrix.

3. Keep the big stretches: compression

Written as a sum, the SVD is a stack of simple layers, each a column times a row, weighted by its singular value:

\[ A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^\top + \dots \]

Each layer \(u_i v_i^\top\) is a rank-1 matrix (every row a multiple of the same row). Keep only the \(k\) biggest and you have the best possible rank-\(k\) approximation of \(A\): no other choice of \(k\) layers comes closer (the Eckart–Young theorem). A grayscale photo is a matrix of brightnesses, so here is Grace Hopper, the computing pioneer, in 1984 (U.S. Navy photo, public domain), rebuilt from her first few layers:

A rank-10 version of a 300 × 256 image stores 10 columns of \(U\) (300 numbers each), 10 rows of \(V^\top\) (256 each), and 10 singular values. How many numbers is that?

\(10 \times (300 + 256 + 1) = 3{,}000 + 2{,}560 + 10 = 5{,}570\), against 76,800 pixels in the original: about 7%, and already recognizable.

4. PCA, done with the SVD

Linear algebra 2 found principal components as eigenvectors of the covariance matrix \(X^\top X/(n-1)\) of centered data. The SVD of the centered data itself gives the same directions without ever forming \(X^\top X\) (which squares the condition number, and loses precision): the rows of \(V^\top\) are the components, and \(\sigma_i^2/(n-1)\) are the variances along them. This is how scikit-learn's PCA does it.

5. Hidden tastes: a tiny recommender

Eight people rated ten films, 1 to 5 stars, but 30% of the ratings are missing. The table is made up from two hidden tastes (how much each film is an action film or a love story, how much each person likes each), plus noise. Nobody tells the SVD that; it finds two big singular values and much smaller ones after them. To guess the missing ratings: fill them with each film's average, take the rank-2 version, put its guesses into the gaps, and repeat. This is the idea behind the Netflix Prize-era recommenders, and still behind many today.

The directions the SVD finds are mixtures you name afterwards ("taste 1 is mostly action against romance"). Real recommenders add regularization and learn from millions of ratings, but the shape of the answer is this one: a person is a short vector of tastes, a film is a short vector of qualities, and a rating is roughly their dot product.

6. Solving the unsolvable: the pseudo-inverse

Linear algebra 1 solved square, invertible systems, and noted that np.linalg.lstsq "uses an SVD". Here's why: invert each stretch, \(\sigma_i \to 1/\sigma_i\), and skip the ones that are zero (or tiny). That's the pseudo-inverse \(A^+ = V\,\Sigma^+\,U^\top\). It exists for every matrix; for an overdetermined system it gives the least-squares answer, and when columns repeat (linear algebra 1's twin column) it still gives one sensible answer, the smallest weights that fit.

7. Why big models fine-tune with low rank

A large language model's layer can be a 4,096 × 4,096 matrix: 16.8 million weights. Fine-tuning it for a new task changes those weights by some \(\Delta W\), and experiments found that useful changes are close to low-rank: a few directions matter. LoRA (stage 6) trains the change directly as a product \(\Delta W = B A\) with \(B\) of shape 4,096 × \(r\) and \(A\) of shape \(r\) × 4,096, for a small rank \(r\) like 8. That's the SVD's lesson used in reverse: if only a few layers matter, learn only those.

With \(r = 8\): what percent of the 16.8 million weights does LoRA train for that layer? (Two decimal places.)

\(2 \times 4096 \times 8 = 65{,}536\) trainable numbers, against \(4096^2 = 16{,}777{,}216\): 0.39%.

8. What to carry forward

  • Every matrix is rotate, stretch, rotate: \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\). The singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots\) are the stretches.
  • \(\sigma_i^2\) are the eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\); for symmetric matrices, singular values are the eigenvalues' sizes. \(\sigma_1/\sigma_{\min}\) is the condition number.
  • Keeping the \(k\) biggest layers gives the best rank-\(k\) approximation: compression, denoising, and the "few hidden factors" behind recommenders.
  • PCA is the SVD of centered data; the pseudo-inverse inverts the stretches it can and skips the rest.
  • Low-rank is everywhere in big models: LoRA trains a fine-tuning update as a thin product.
