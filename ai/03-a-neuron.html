A neuron from scratch

An artificial neuron is almost embarrassingly simple: multiply each input by a weight, add them up with a bias, and push the total through a bend. That's it. Yet put enough of them together and they recognize faces and write code. Let's build one, look at what it sees, and watch it learn.

1. A weighted sum and a bend

Our neuron looks at a point with two numbers, (x₁, x₂). It computes

z = w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + b   →   output = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

The bend σ (the sigmoid) squashes any number into 0…1, so the output reads like "how sure am I that this point is blue?". The two weights form an arrow, w. Drag its tip, slide the bias, and drag the green probe point.

Look at the shading: the neuron splits the plane with a straight line, where z = 0 and it's exactly 50% sure. That line is always perpendicular to the weight arrow. The arrow points toward "more blue", its length says how sharp the switch is (a long arrow means a crisp edge, a short one a fuzzy blur), and the bias slides the line back and forth without turning it. Remember chapter 1: w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ is a matrix with one row. A neuron is a tiny layer.

2. Watch it learn

Here are two clouds of points. We want the neuron to say "blue" for blue points and "red" for red ones. Its loss (how wrong it is) is the log loss: for each point, how surprised the neuron is by the right answer. Training is chapter 2's loop: compute the gradient of the loss with respect to w₁, w₂, b, step downhill, repeat.

Drag points around · click empty space to add a blue point, shift-click (or long-press) for red

Notice what the gradient does here, in plain words: every misclassified point pulls the line toward itself and pushes the weight arrow toward its own side. Points the neuron already gets right, with confidence, barely pull at all. That's the whole of learning for one neuron: lots of small nudges from its mistakes.

3. What one neuron can't do

Now a pattern a child can see: blue in two opposite corners, red in the other two (it's called XOR). Train the single neuron on it.

It can't. However it turns its line, one straight cut can't put opposite corners together, so it gets stuck around half right. This exact problem stalled neural network research for a decade in the 1970s. The fix is the one from chapter 1: stack layers. Four neurons in a hidden layer each make their own cut; a final neuron combines their answers, and now the boundary can bend around the corners. Press the second button and watch it curve.

But how did that hidden layer learn? Each of its neurons had to figure out its share of the blame for the final answer, through the neuron after it. Passing blame backwards like that is backpropagation: the next chapter.
