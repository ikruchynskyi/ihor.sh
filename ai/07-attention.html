Attention and transformers

Every chatbot you've used is a transformer: a stack of two operations repeated dozens of times. One of them is chapter 3's familiar layer. The other, attention, is the new idea, and it's what lets a model understand that "bank" means something different next to "river" than next to "loan". Here's attention with all the numbers visible.

1. Words are arrows

A model first turns every word into a vector, its embedding: a list of numbers learned so that words used in similar ways end up close together. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of directions; here are 16 words in just two, so we can see them. Directions in this space mean something: going from man to woman is about the same move as from king to queen.

− + ≈

Drag words around and the analogy updates. In real models these directions aren't placed by hand; they fall out of training on billions of sentences, because predicting the next word well requires knowing that queens and kings are alike in most ways and different in one.

The math, step by step

A vocabulary of \(V\) words and vectors of size \(d\) make an embedding matrix \(E\) with \(V\) rows and \(d\) columns. "Looking up" word number \(k\) is just taking row \(k\). (Formally: multiply a one-hot vector, all zeros except a 1 at \(k\), by \(E\). It's a matrix like any other, so backprop trains it like any other.) GPT-2 used \(V = 50{,}257\) and \(d = 768\): 38.6 million numbers just for this table.

"Close together" is measured by the angle, with cosine similarity, chapter 1's dot product divided by the lengths:

\[ \cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert\, \lVert b \rVert} \in [-1, 1] \]

The analogy is plain vector arithmetic: compute \(\text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman}\), then find the word whose embedding has the highest cosine with the result (leaving out the three input words, which are usually closest).

Two embeddings point in exactly opposite directions. What is their cosine similarity?

  • 0
  • −1
  • 1
\(\cos 180^\circ = -1\). In practice opposite meanings rarely end up opposite; "hot" and "cold" are used in very similar sentences, so their embeddings are quite close.

2. Attention: let the other words weigh in

An embedding is the same for "bank" everywhere. Attention fixes that. Each word asks a question (its query), every word offers an answer tag (its key), and how well they match decides how much attention is paid. The word's new meaning is a weighted average of what the others offer (their values), added to its own. Click a word.

Watch "bank": next to river, its new position slides toward the nature side; next to loan, toward money. Little words like "the" have small vectors, so they barely get attention. The math in the table is chapter 5 again: scores are dot products of query and key (how much they point the same way), divided by √(size), then softmax turns them into weights that add up to 1.

Now tick the causal mask. A language model writes left to right, so a word may only look at words before it. In "i sat by the bank of the river", "bank" can't see "river" yet and stays ambiguous. The later words carry the context forward instead, and that's one reason models need many layers.

The math, step by step

Stack the sentence's \(n\) embeddings as rows of a matrix \(X\) (\(n \times d\)). Three learned matrices turn each word into its query, key and value:

\[ Q = X W_Q, \quad K = X W_K, \quad V = X W_V \]

Then every query is compared with every key (an \(n \times n\) table of dot products), scaled, turned into weights by softmax row by row, and used to average the values:

\[ \operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left( \frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) V \]

Why \(\sqrt{d_k}\)? If query and key have \(d_k\) independent random entries of size about 1, their dot product is a sum of \(d_k\) random terms, so its typical size grows like \(\sqrt{d_k}\). Without the division, big models would feed softmax huge scores, it would put all the weight on one word, and its gradient (chapter 5) would vanish. Dividing keeps scores around size 1 at any \(d_k\).

The causal mask sets the score for every later word to \(-\infty\) before the softmax; \(e^{-\infty} = 0\), so those words get exactly zero weight.

Cost. The \(QK^\top\) table has \(n^2\) entries: double the context, four times the work. That's why long context windows are expensive.

A head with \(d_k = 64\). What number are the scores divided by?

\(\sqrt{64} = 8\).

With the causal mask on, how does the first word of a sentence attend?

  • 100% to itself: it's the only word it may see
  • Evenly to all words
  • It gets no attention output at all
Its row of scores has one finite entry (itself) and \(-\infty\) everywhere else, so softmax gives \((1, 0, 0, \dots)\).

3. A transformer is attention + a layer, stacked

words → embeddings (+ position)
↓ ×96 in a big model
attention: mix in context → add & normalize → layer from ch. 3: think per word → add & normalize
↓
last word's vector → scores for every word in the vocabulary → softmax (ch. 5) → sample the next word

That's the whole architecture. Each block lets words exchange information (attention), then lets each word think on its own (the per-word layer: matrices and bends, chapter 1). Real models run many attention heads in parallel per block, each with its own query, key and value matrices, so one head can track grammar while another tracks who "he" refers to. Everything is trained exactly as before: predict the next word, measure the surprise (chapter 5), backprop the blame (chapter 4), step downhill (chapter 2), on trillions of words.

The math: one block, and where the parameters live

One block, for a sequence \(X\) of \(n\) vectors of size \(d\):

\[ H = X + \operatorname{Attention}(\operatorname{LN}(X)) \qquad Y = H + W_2\, \operatorname{GELU}(W_1\, \operatorname{LN}(H)) \]

The \(X + \dots\) parts are residual connections: each block adds a correction to its input instead of replacing it, so blame flows straight back through dozens of blocks without fading. LN is layer norm: shift and scale each vector to mean 0 and spread 1, which keeps numbers in a range where training is stable. GELU is a smooth ReLU.

Parameter count per block: \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) and an output matrix are each \(d \times d\): \(4d^2\). The per-word layer goes from \(d\) up to \(4d\) and back: \(2 \cdot 4d^2 = 8d^2\). About \(12 d^2\) per block. GPT-2 small (\(d = 768\), 12 blocks): \(12 \cdot 12 \cdot 768^2 \approx 85\) million, plus the 38.6 million embedding table: 124 million.

About how many parameters are in one block with \(d = 768\), using \(12 d^2\)?

\(12 \times 768^2 = 12 \times 589{,}824 = 7{,}077{,}888\).
