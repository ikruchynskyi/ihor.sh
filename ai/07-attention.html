Attention and transformers

Every chatbot you've used is a transformer: a stack of two operations repeated dozens of times. One of them is chapter 3's familiar layer. The other, attention, is the new idea, and it's what lets a model understand that "bank" means something different next to "river" than next to "loan". Here's attention with all the numbers visible.

1. Words are arrows

A model first turns every word into a vector, its embedding: a list of numbers learned so that words used in similar ways end up close together. Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of directions; here are 16 words in just two, so we can see them. Directions in this space mean something: going from man to woman is about the same move as from king to queen.

− + ≈

Drag words around and the analogy updates. In real models these directions aren't placed by hand; they fall out of training on billions of sentences, because predicting the next word well requires knowing that queens and kings are alike in most ways and different in one.

2. Attention: let the other words weigh in

An embedding is the same for "bank" everywhere. Attention fixes that. Each word asks a question (its query), every word offers an answer tag (its key), and how well they match decides how much attention is paid. The word's new meaning is a weighted average of what the others offer (their values), added to its own. Click a word.

Watch "bank": next to river, its new position slides toward the nature side; next to loan, toward money. Little words like "the" have small vectors, so they barely get attention. The math in the table is chapter 5 again: scores are dot products of query and key (how much they point the same way), divided by √(size), then softmax turns them into weights that add up to 1.

Now tick the causal mask. A language model writes left to right, so a word may only look at words before it. In "i sat by the bank of the river", "bank" can't see "river" yet and stays ambiguous. The later words carry the context forward instead, and that's one reason models need many layers.

3. A transformer is attention + a layer, stacked

words → embeddings (+ position)
↓ ×96 in a big model
attention: mix in context → add & normalize → layer from ch. 3: think per word → add & normalize
↓
last word's vector → scores for every word in the vocabulary → softmax (ch. 5) → sample the next word

That's the whole architecture. Each block lets words exchange information (attention), then lets each word think on its own (the per-word layer: matrices and bends, chapter 1). Real models run many attention heads in parallel per block, each with its own query, key and value matrices, so one head can track grammar while another tracks who "he" refers to. Everything is trained exactly as before: predict the next word, measure the surprise (chapter 5), backprop the blame (chapter 4), step downhill (chapter 2), on trillions of words.
