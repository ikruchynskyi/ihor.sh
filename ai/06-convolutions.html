AI from scratch · Chapter 6

Seeing: convolutions

A photo of a cat and the same photo shifted one pixel to the left are, number by number, completely different. Yet they're obviously the same cat. A plain network from chapter 3 would have to learn "cat" separately at every position. Convolutional networks don't: they slide one small detector across the whole image. This chapter builds one and trains it, right here in your browser, to read digits you draw.

1. A picture is a grid of numbers

Draw a digit in the box. To a network it's 28 × 28 = 784 numbers between 0 (black) and 1 (white), the same size as the classic MNIST dataset of handwritten digits. Hover over the small copy to see the numbers.

Hover over the 28 × 28 image to read its pixels.

2. A filter slides over it

A filter (or kernel) is a tiny grid of weights, here 3 × 3. Put it on a spot of the image, multiply the 9 pixels under it by its 9 weights, add them up: that's one output number. Slide it to every position and you get a new image, the feature map. The filter below finds edges where the image goes from dark (left) to bright (right). Pick others, and hover the output to see the arithmetic.

Hover over the feature map.

The same 9 weights are used everywhere, so the filter finds a vertical edge wherever it is. That's the trick: weight sharing. A network layer has many filters side by side, each looking for its own pattern, and the next layer's filters look at those maps, finding edges of edges: corners, loops, strokes, and eventually whole shapes. Between layers a pooling step keeps only the strongest response in each 2 × 2 patch, so the network cares less about exactly where a stroke is.

3. Train a network that reads digits

Our network: 8 filters of 5 × 5 (learned, not hand-picked) → ReLU → 2 × 2 max pooling → one dense layer of 10 outputs → softmax (chapter 5). About 12,000 weights. It trains on digits drawn in your computer's fonts, randomly rotated, scaled and thickened, with backprop (chapter 4) written out by hand in this page. Press train and watch the filters grow from noise into stroke detectors.

4. Draw, and it reads

Draw a digit in the box at the top (or below, it's the same drawing) and the network gives its probabilities. Draw big, roughly centered, with one continuous shape like the printed digits it learned from.

Train the network first (section 3).

It will make mistakes, especially on digits drawn very differently from fonts: it has only ever seen typefaces. Real digit readers train on tens of thousands of real handwritten samples (MNIST has 60,000), with more layers. But it's the same machine: filters that slide, pooling that forgives, a softmax that votes, and gradient descent that tunes every weight.
