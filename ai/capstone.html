Track A · Predict broken elevators, live

For a rider in a wheelchair or with a stroller, an out-of-service elevator decides the whole trip. The MTA publishes which elevators and escalators are out and a time it expects them back, and that estimate is longer than the real outage 98% of the time (toolkit 2). A better answer to "will this still be broken in four hours?" would help people plan. This capstone builds it, end to end, on this site's live archive: data, model, honest evaluation, a service, and monitoring. Below is the course's own baseline running on the outages happening right now; your job is to beat it and ship yours.

1. The live board

Every elevator and escalator out of service in the MTA's latest feed, scored by a logistic regression trained on 595 finished outages (toolkit 2's model, exported as 14 weights and 6 scaling numbers). The probability is the model's chance that the outage lasts more than 4 hours from its start. Come back later and see which were right.

Two things the board shows that a notebook hides. Some outages have a reason the model never saw in training ("Capital Replacement": escalators being rebuilt for months); the model treats an unknown category as "nothing known" and its guess means little. And many outages are already past 4 hours: the question for them is settled, which leads to this project's central trap.

The baseline already runs on live data; the capstone is to build a better one and run it the same way, honestly measured.

Why the unfinished outages bias everything: censoring
  1. To train, you need each outage's length. You only know it for outages that have ended. The rest are right-censored: you know they've lasted at least this long, not how long.
  2. Long outages are more likely to still be open when you take a snapshot, simply because they're open longer. In the archive: 27% of finished outages lasted over 4 hours, but 54% of the ones still open have already passed 4 hours.
  3. So training on finished outages only under-represents long ones, and the model learns that outages are shorter than they are. It gets worse the more recent the data: the newest week has the most unfinished long outages.
  4. One fix for this task: an outage that's open and already past 4 hours has a known label (yes), so include it; only the ones open for less than 4 hours are truly unknown. More generally, survival analysis (Kaplan-Meier curves, Cox models) is built for censored durations.
  5. The same trap appears whenever the label takes time: customer churn, loan default, how long a job posting stays open.

2. Track A Milestones

  1. Data. Load outages.csv (or, better, ask for a fresh export of the archive: it grows every day). Look first (toolkit 2): ranges, the two-year-old capital replacements, reasons, duplicates. Decide what to do with censored outages (above). Deliverable: a cleaned table and a paragraph on what you dropped and why.
  2. Baseline and features. The baseline to beat is the board's model (time-split AUC about 0.89). Ideas: the station's history (careful: compute it from the past only, features and leakage), time of day and weekday, the MTA's own estimate (but only the first one it published; the archive keeps the latest, which may have been updated after the fact: leakage), the number of lines. Deliverable: a feature list with, for each, "known at the moment of prediction?".
  3. Honest evaluation. A time split, never random (features and leakage). AUC and a precision-recall curve (chapter 12), a calibration check if you'll show probabilities, and a threshold chosen from costs: a rider told "long" who reroutes needlessly loses minutes; one told "short" who arrives at a dead elevator loses far more. Compare with the baseline on the same test days, paired (Probability 4). Deliverable: one table, one curve, one threshold with its reason.
  4. Model card. Intended use, data, evaluation (overall and per slice: elevators vs escalators, accessible stations), limitations (MLOps). Deliverable: the card.
  5. Service. FastAPI around the model, inputs validated, version returned (serving); or export it to JSON like the board's and score in the browser. Free hosting: a Hugging Face Space (Start 4). Deliverable: a URL.
  6. Monitoring. Log every prediction with its inputs. Each day, when outages end, score yesterday's predictions; track the reason mix's PSI against the training window, weekday against weekday (MLOps). Deliverable: a week of daily AUC and PSI.
  7. Write-up. One page: the problem, what worked, what didn't, what you'd do with more data. Link the notebook, the service and the card. That page is your portfolio piece.

A teammate wants to add the MTA's estimated return time from the archive as a feature: it lifts test AUC to 0.97. What do you check first?

  • Nothing: ship it
  • Whether more data makes it even better
  • Whether that estimate was known when the outage started: the archive stores the latest estimate, which may have been revised after the outage was already long
Features and leakage's question for every column: would I know this value at the moment the model has to decide? A revised estimate carries information from the future. Use only the first estimate the feed published.

Seven milestones, each with a deliverable: data, features known in time, honest evaluation, a model card, a live service, a week of monitoring, a one-page write-up.

Where it breaks: a week of data, and a model riders might trust too much
  • One week is one week. The archive's finished outages mostly started in a single week of October. Holidays, winter storms and summer heat are all unseen. Say so in the card, and keep collecting: the MLOps lesson's monitoring is also how the training set grows.
  • Unseen categories. A new reason, station or equipment type gets no learned weight. Flag those predictions as low-confidence (the board does), rather than showing a number.
  • False confidence. "93% chance it's back within 4 hours" can send a wheelchair user to a dead elevator 7% of the time. For accessibility, a cautious threshold and plain words ("often fixed within hours, but plan an alternative") may serve riders better than a precise-looking probability.

3. Run the Full Copilot Pipeline in Python

Run the complete 7-stage reference implementation right in your browser: SQLite window-function deduplication, multimodal Whisper/OCR parsing, asymmetric cost threshold search, 2D SVD projection, TF-IDF cosine retrieval, token-overlap grounding audit, and PSI drift detection.

Why asymmetric costs shift the optimal cutoff to \(P(y = 1 \mid x) \ge \frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FN}} + C_{\text{FP}}}\)

Suppose a document has true calibrated probability \(p = P(y = 1 \mid x)\) of being an urgent incident:

  1. If we predict urgent (\(\hat y = 1\)), we only pay when the document is actually routine (\(y = 0\)), which happens with probability \(1 - p\). Expected cost of predicting 1: \((1 - p)\,C_{\text{FP}}\).
  2. If we predict routine (\(\hat y = 0\)), we only pay when the document is actually an incident (\(y = 1\)), which happens with probability \(p\). Expected cost of predicting 0: \(p\,C_{\text{FN}}\).
  3. Predicting \(\hat y = 1\) is cheaper whenever \((1 - p)\,C_{\text{FP}} \le p\,C_{\text{FN}}\), which rearranges to \(p\,(C_{\text{FN}} + C_{\text{FP}}) \ge C_{\text{FP}}\), or \(p \ge \frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FN}} + C_{\text{FP}}}\).
  4. When \(C_{\text{FN}} = C_{\text{FP}} = 1\), the theoretical cutoff is \(\frac{1}{1+1} = 0.50\). When missing an outage costs \(C_{\text{FN}} = 10\) and a false alarm costs \(C_{\text{FP}} = 1\), the theoretical Bayes cutoff drops to \(\frac{1}{10+1} \approx 0.091\) (and on our finite training grid, any threshold in \([0.10, 0.30]\) achieves zero total cost).
Where it breaks: lexical grounding blind spots and non-stationary drift baselines
  • Negation and paraphrase in token overlap. A token-overlap grounding scorer checks whether content words in the answer appear in the retrieved chunk. Notice that "The router did NOT fail due to memory exhaustion under spike load [doc-2#1]" shares 100% of its >3-character content words with doc-2#1, yet states the exact opposite! Pair token overlap with the NLI entailment/contradiction filter from Evaluating LLM Apps to catch inverted claims.
  • Planned traffic shifts look like drift. On Monday mornings or during a scheduled chaos-engineering drill, urgent incident queries spike legitimately, pushing \(\text{PSI} > 0.25\). Compare production windows against matching day-of-week baselines (MLOps) before triggering an automated retrain.

4. Track B Milestones

  1. Data engineering & SQL window functions. Set up an SQLite relational store for documents and multi-chunk embeddings. Write a window query using ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY doc_id ORDER BY chunk_seq ASC) to isolate starting chunk states without temporal leakage. Deliverable: SQLite schema and executed query.
  2. Multimodal voice & receipt OCR ingestion. Strip Whisper [MM:SS] timestamps and spoken fillers from voice memos, and parse structured fields (VENDOR, ITEM, TOTAL) from receipt OCR scans before inserting into SQLite. Deliverable: multimodal parser and ingestion check.
  3. Cost-aware tabular triage. Fit a logistic regression on metadata features [word_count, is_incident, created_hour]. Tune the decision threshold against an asymmetric cost matrix ($C(\text{FN}) = 10, C(\text{FP}) = 1$). Deliverable: optimal decision threshold minimizing total enterprise loss.
  4. PyTorch neural bi-encoder & 2D SVD semantic map. Build a custom BiEncoderHead(nn.Module) projecting vocabulary representations into 16D unit-normalized embeddings ($L_2$ norm = 1.0), and project centered TF-IDF vectors onto their top 2 singular vectors ($\mathbf{X}_c \mathbf{V}_{:,:2}$) for visual exploration. Deliverable: PyTorch module and 2D SVD projection.
  5. Cosine vector retrieval & grounded provenance. Compute dot products $\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{D}^\top$, retrieve the top-$k$ relevant chunks descending by cosine similarity, and format prompts with strict bracketed citations: [{doc_id}#{chunk_seq}] {text}. Deliverable: vector retrieval and formatted context string.
  6. Automated grounding evaluation. Build an algorithmic hallucination detector calculating the token overlap ratio between candidate answer statements and context text. Deliverable: grounding ratio scorer rejecting statements below 75% overlap.
  7. FastAPI serving, PSI drift monitoring & Model Card. Wrap the pipeline in a service handler returning latency measurements under 100 ms, compute the Population Stability Index (PSI) across incoming traffic to trigger alerts when $\text{PSI} > 0.25$, and publish a complete Model Card. Deliverable: FastAPI service handler, PSI drift detector, and Model Card.

Why do we tune the triage classifier's threshold down to 0.30 instead of using the standard 0.50 cutoff?

  • To maximize standard raw classification accuracy
  • Because missing an urgent incident (False Negative) costs 10x more than reviewing a false alarm (False Positive)
  • To make PyTorch matrix multiplication faster
In real systems, mistake costs are asymmetric. Missing a critical Sev-1 production outage (FN) means downtime and SLA penalties; reviewing a routine ticket flagged as urgent (FP) costs a few seconds of engineer triage. A lower threshold trades a few harmless false alarms to eliminate costly missed outages.
