Confidence and tests: A/B testing

Your new model scores 85% on the test set; the old one scored 82%. Ship it? Maybe the test set happened to suit it. Every number measured on a sample wobbles, and this lesson is about how much: confidence intervals that say how far off an estimate might be, tests that ask whether a difference is real or luck, and the sample sizes that make the answer trustworthy. These are the tools behind every A/B test in production and every "B beats A" claim in a paper.

1. A confidence interval, and what "95%" means

Estimate a mean from \(n\) data points and the result wobbles from sample to sample, with spread \(\sigma / \sqrt n\) (Probability 3). So report a range: the 95% confidence interval

\[ \bar x \pm t \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt n} \]

where \(s\) is the sample standard deviation and \(t\) is about 2 (exactly 1.96 for big samples; section 2 says why it's bigger for small ones). The term after \(\pm\) is the margin of error. Below, the computer takes 100 samples from a population whose true mean is the dashed line, and draws each sample's interval. Green ones caught the truth, red ones missed.

That's the honest meaning of 95%: the method catches the truth 95% of the time. Any single interval either contains the true mean or doesn't; you just don't know which. Bigger \(n\) makes intervals narrower, but the share that miss stays the same.

A sample of 100 has mean 50 and standard deviation 10. What's the 95% margin of error (use 1.96)?

\(1.96 \times 10 / \sqrt{100} = 1.96\). The interval is 48.04 to 51.96. To halve the margin you'd need four times the data.

Which statement about a 95% interval [48.0, 52.0] is right?

  • There's a 95% chance the true mean is between 48 and 52
  • If we repeated the sampling many times, 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true mean
  • 95% of the data lies between 48 and 52
Subtle but it matters. (A Bayesian credible interval, from Probability 3's posterior, does mean "95% probability the value is in here", given the prior. With lots of data the two usually agree in practice.)

2. Small samples need the t-distribution

Tick use 1.96 instead of t with \(n = 5\): about one interval in eight misses instead of one in twenty. With few points, \(s\) itself is a shaky estimate of \(\sigma\), and sometimes it comes out too small, so the intervals need to be wider. The t-distribution (found by a Guinness brewer, W. S. Gosset, testing small batches of barley) is the bell curve with fatter tails that accounts for that. It has degrees of freedom \(n - 1\); by 30 or so it's nearly normal, and its 95% cutoff is close to 1.96.

3. A test: could it be luck?

A hypothesis test asks one specific question. Start from the boring explanation, the null hypothesis \(H_0\): "there's no difference; any gap is sampling noise". Then compute the p-value: if \(H_0\) were true, how often would noise alone produce a gap at least as big as the one you saw? A small p-value (by convention below 0.05) means the gap would be surprising under the null, so you reject it.

The math, step by step

Unpaired (two separate test sets): the difference of two proportions has standard error \(\sqrt{p_A(1 - p_A)/n + p_B(1 - p_B)/n}\); divide the gap by it to get \(z\), and the p-value is the area of both bell tails beyond \(\pm z\).

Paired (one test set, the right way for models): examples both models get right, or both wrong, say nothing about which is better. Only the disagreements count. Let \(b\) = A right and B wrong, \(c\) = A wrong and B right. Under \(H_0\) each disagreement is a coin flip, so (McNemar's test)

\[ z = \frac{c - b}{\sqrt{b + c}} \]

With 500 examples, 90% agreement means 50 disagreements; B's 3-point edge is 15 more of them going B's way: \(c = 32.5,\ b = 17.5\), \(z = 15/\sqrt{50} \approx 2.12\), \(p \approx 0.034\). The unpaired test on the same numbers gives \(z \approx 1.28\), \(p \approx 0.20\): it can't see past the shared noise.

What does \(p = 0.034\) mean here?

  • If the two models were truly equally good, a gap this big in B's favor (or A's) would show up only about 3.4% of the time
  • There's a 3.4% chance the models are equally good
  • B is definitely 3 points better
And "significant" isn't "important": with a million test examples, a 0.1-point gap is significant and probably irrelevant. Always report the size of the difference with its confidence interval, not just p.

Chapter 12's confusion matrix, for decisions about models. The threshold \(\alpha = 0.05\) caps false positives. Power \(= 1 - \beta\) is the chance of detecting a real effect, and it depends on the effect's size, the noise, and \(n\). The blue bell is what the measured difference looks like with no real effect; the yellow one with a real effect. Everything right of the red line is "significant".

Before a test, you pick \(\alpha\), the smallest effect worth detecting, and the power you want (80% is the custom), and that fixes \(n\). For comparing two proportions at \(\alpha = 0.05\) and 80% power, a handy rule:

\[ n \text{ per group} \approx \frac{16\, p(1 - p)}{\delta^2} \]

A sign-up page converts 10% of visitors. To detect a lift to 11% (\(\delta = 0.01\)), about how many visitors does each version need?

\(16 \times 0.1 \times 0.9 / 0.01^2 = 14{,}400\) per version. Small effects on rare events need big samples, which is why A/B tests run for weeks, and why "we tried it on 200 users and it looked better" means little.

5. Peeking ruins the test

The 5% false-positive promise holds for one look at a pre-planned sample size. Checking an A/B test every day and stopping the moment \(p < 0.05\) gives noise many chances to cross the line. The same trap shows up in ML. You try 20 hyperparameter settings and report the best one's test score. What's wrong?

  • Nothing, as long as each run used the same test set
  • You should have tried more settings
  • Picking the best of 20 on the test set is 20 peeks: its score is optimistic, like a lucky A/B winner
That's why chapter 11 keeps a validation set for choosing and touches the test set once. With many comparisons, either hold out fresh data for the final check or correct the threshold (Bonferroni: use \(\alpha / k\) for \(k\) comparisons).

6. A/B tests in production

Offline test sets only go so far: the real question is whether users are better off. An online A/B test randomly splits traffic between the current model (A) and the new one (B) and compares a business metric. The checklist:

  1. Decide first: the metric, the smallest effect worth shipping, \(\alpha\), power, and so \(n\) and the duration. Write it down.
  2. Randomize by user, not by request, so one person sees one version.
  3. Check the split: if you meant 50/50 and got 50.8/49.2 on a million users, something's broken (a "sample ratio mismatch").
  4. Run full weeks: behavior differs on weekends; novelty wears off.
  5. Don't peek to stop (or use methods designed for it, like sequential tests).
  6. Report the effect with its interval, plus guardrail metrics (latency, errors, complaints) that must not get worse.

Two models are scored on the same 500 test questions. Which test fits?

  • A two-sample test of proportions
  • A paired test on the disagreements (McNemar), or a paired bootstrap
The same questions are easy or hard for both models, and pairing cancels that shared difficulty, as the section 3 picture showed. Chapter 8's Blip eval compares models exactly this way.

7. What to carry forward

  • A confidence interval \(\bar x \pm t\, s/\sqrt n\) catches the truth in 95% of samples; the 95% describes the method, not one interval.
  • Small samples need the t-distribution's wider cutoffs.
  • A p-value is how often noise alone would produce a gap this big if there were no real difference. It's not the chance the null is true, and significant isn't the same as important.
  • Type I = false alarm (\(\alpha\)); Type II = missed effect (\(\beta\)); power = \(1 - \beta\) grows with effect size and \(n\). Plan \(n\) before you start.
  • Compare models on the same test set with a paired test. Don't peek and stop; many comparisons need a stricter threshold or fresh data.
