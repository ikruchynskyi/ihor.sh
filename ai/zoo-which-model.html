Which model when: the bake-off

Nine pages, nine models, each with its picture, its math and its failure modes. This page puts them side by side: first on the same toy maps, where you can see each model's built-in assumptions as shapes, then on four real datasets from this course, with scores, training times and sizes. The results are less tidy than the folklore, and the reasons why are the most useful thing on the page.

Logistic regression beat boosted trees on the elevator outages by 0.019 AUC on 321 test outages. How confident should you be that it's really better there?

  • Very: 0.019 is a clear win
  • Not at all: they're identical
  • Not very: a bootstrap of the test outages puts the difference anywhere from −0.013 to +0.055
Resampling the 321 test outages 1,000 times (probability lesson 4's bootstrap), the 95% interval for "logistic minus boosting" was −0.013 to +0.055; on the 2,575 bank test calls, 0.000 to 0.037. So the line is at least as good, and maybe better, but a single split can't rank models that are this close. Report intervals, use cross-validation, and decide on other grounds (readability, size, speed) when scores tie.

3. What the table says

  • No model wins everywhere. The line won two datasets, the SVM the other two, and boosting, the folklore champion, won none, while never being far off (2nd, 2nd, 4th, 6th).
  • Small data favors strong assumptions. All four datasets are small (300 to 8,000 training rows). Simple models with the right built-in assumption (effects add up for the bank calls; distance between images means similarity for digits) need less data to learn it. Boosting's edge grows with rows and interactions, which these sets don't have much of.
  • The data's shape picks the winner. Ridership has a level that keeps shifting: trees and forests can't predict outside the levels they saw (0.34 to 0.38), the line overshoots its trend (0.37), and the models that combine smoothly (SVM 0.24, MLP 0.27) adapted best.
  • Costs differ by 1,000×. A forest weighs 25 MB where a line weighs 4 kB; k-NN carries its whole training set. When scores tie, that decides.

Choose by the data's shape, its size and what you need besides accuracy, start with a simple baseline, and don't trust a ranking from one split when the scores are close.

Why no model can win everywhere (no free lunch)
  1. A model only sees the training rows. For any row it hasn't seen, the training data alone doesn't say what the label is: many rules fit the same training data and disagree off it.
  2. Every model breaks that tie with built-in assumptions: "nearby points share labels" (k-NN, SVM), "effects add up" (linear), "the answer changes in steps along columns" (trees).
  3. Average over every possible way the unseen labels could go, and each assumption is right exactly as often as it's wrong: every model has the same average accuracy off the training data (Wolpert, 1996).
  4. So "the best model" only means something for a kind of problem. Real problems aren't random, they have structure, and a model wins when its assumptions match that structure.
  5. That's the practical method: look at the data (is it a table, an image, a time series? how many rows? are effects additive?) and pick the models whose assumptions fit, then let validation data decide among them.

Where it breaks: how bake-offs mislead
  • One split. As measured above, differences of 0.02 AUC on a few hundred test rows are within noise. Use cross-validation and report intervals (probability lesson 4).
  • Defaults against tuned. XGBoost went from 0.744 to 0.774 with tuning (page 7), more than the gap between most models here. Compare models at equal tuning effort, or say you didn't tune.
  • Test reuse. Every look at the test set to pick a model leaks a little of it into your choice. Pick on validation data; touch the test set once.
  • The wrong metric. AUC ranks; it doesn't check calibration (naive Bayes ranks well with bad probabilities) or cost (chapter 12). Score what the decision needs.
  • Leaderboards. Many teams trying many models on one public test set will find one that's lucky on it. A model that ranks first by 0.002 usually isn't first on the next data.
