Measuring a model

"It's 99% accurate" is the most common way an ML project fools its own team. A model's quality isn't one number: it depends on how rare the thing you're looking for is, where you draw the line between yes and no, and what each kind of mistake costs. This chapter is the vocabulary for saying what a classifier is actually good for.

1. A classifier gives scores, not answers

Say a model flags fraudulent card payments. For each payment it outputs a score; we say "fraud" when the score is over a threshold. Below are 10,000 payments: the honest ones' scores and the frauds' scores. A good model pushes them apart; they always overlap somewhere.

With fraud at 1%, a "model" that says never fraud scores 99% accuracy and catches nothing. Accuracy counts the easy majority; it hides the cases you built the model for. So we split the mistakes:

99%: it's right about every honest payment. That's why accuracy alone says almost nothing about rare events.

2. Precision and recall

Recall (sensitivity): of all the frauds, how many did we flag? Precision: of everything we flagged, how much was really fraud? Move the threshold above: down, and recall rises while precision falls (we flag more honest people); up, the opposite. There's no setting that's free.

\[ \text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP} \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN} \qquad F_1 = \frac{2\,\text{precision}\cdot\text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}} \]

Now make fraud rarer with the middle slider and keep the threshold. Recall doesn't change (it's measured only on frauds) but precision collapses: the honest crowd is so large that even a small false-positive rate buries the real hits. This is the base-rate trap behind every "the test is 99% accurate, so…" mistake.

A screening model has a false-positive rate of 1% and catches 90% of cases. The condition affects 1 in 1,000. Roughly what fraction of flagged people actually have it?

  • About 90%
  • About 8%
  • About 0.1%
Per 100,000 people: 100 have it, 90 are flagged. Of the 99,900 who don't, 1% (999) are flagged too. Precision = 90 / (90 + 999) ≈ 8%.

3. Every threshold at once: ROC and precision-recall curves

Sweep the threshold from top to bottom and plot what you get at each setting. The ROC curve plots recall (true-positive rate) against the false-positive rate; the precision-recall curve plots what it sounds like. The dot is your current threshold.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) has a lovely meaning: the chance that a random fraud scores higher than a random honest payment. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect separation. It doesn't move when you change how rare fraud is (try it), which makes it good for comparing models and bad for predicting what users will see. For rare events, look at the precision-recall curve: that one shows the trap.

The math: AUC as a ranking probability

With scores \(s^+_i\) for the \(n_+\) positives and \(s^-_j\) for the \(n_-\) negatives,

\[ \text{AUC} = \frac{1}{n_+ n_-} \sum_i \sum_j \mathbf{1}\left[s^+_i > s^-_j\right] \]

the fraction of (positive, negative) pairs ranked the right way round. Sort all scores once and it's a sum of ranks (the Mann–Whitney U statistic), so it costs \(O(n \log n)\), not \(n_+ n_-\). For two bell curves a distance \(d\) apart with the same spread, \(\text{AUC} = \Phi(d / \sqrt 2)\).

4. Choosing the threshold: what does a mistake cost?

The right threshold isn't a property of the model; it's a business decision. A missed fraud might cost $400; blocking an honest customer's card might cost $20 in support time and goodwill. Pick the costs, and the threshold that minimizes the expected cost follows.

When missing is expensive, the best threshold drops (flag more); when false alarms are expensive, it rises. Medical screening, spam filters and fraud teams all pick different points on the same curve.

5. Calibration: does 0.8 mean 80%?

Many models output a probability, and people act on it ("80% chance of rain, take an umbrella"). A model is calibrated if, among all the times it says 0.8, the thing happens 80% of the time. Modern neural networks are often overconfident: their 0.95s come true much less than 95% of the time. The fix from chapter 5: divide the scores by a temperature before the sigmoid, with T chosen on validation data.

The bars show, for each bin of predicted probability, how often fraud really happened. On the diagonal, the model means what it says. The expected calibration error (ECE) is the average gap, weighted by how many predictions fell in each bin; with rare fraud nearly every prediction sits in the lowest bin, so look at the confident ones too (the readout), where overconfidence hurts. Temperature doesn't change the ranking, so the AUC stays exactly the same: calibration and discrimination are different properties.

6. What to report

  • The base rate of the thing you predict, and a baseline (always-no, last week's rule) next to every number.
  • Precision and recall at the threshold you'll ship, and the cost-based reason for that threshold.
  • AUC or the PR curve to compare models independent of the threshold.
  • Calibration, if anyone will read the probabilities.
  • Slices: the same numbers per country, device, customer type. A model that's fine on average can fail a whole group.
