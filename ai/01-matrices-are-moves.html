Matrices are moves

Every neural network, from a digit recognizer to the model behind a chatbot, is mostly one operation repeated billions of times: multiply by a matrix. So before any "AI", let's get a feel for what a matrix does. Not as a grid of numbers, but as a way of moving space. Drag everything.

1. A vector is an arrow

A vector is a pair of numbers, (x, y), but it's easier to think of it as an arrow from the origin: go x steps right, y steps up. Drag the tip of the blue arrow.

In machine learning, everything becomes a vector: a pixel image is a long list of brightness values, a word becomes a list of a few hundred numbers. Same idea, just with more than two directions to walk in.

The pictureAn arrow: how far right, how far up. Its length is how far the tip is from the start.
The mathA list of numbers \(v = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)\). Its length comes from Pythagoras: \(\lVert v \rVert = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \dots + v_n^2}\).

Drag the arrow to (3, 4). How long is it? (Predict first, then check the readout under the picture.)

\(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\). The arrow is the long side of a right triangle with sides 3 and 4.

2. Adding is walking one arrow, then the other

To add two vectors, walk along the first, then from its tip along the second. Where you end up is the sum. Drag both tips.

Notice you can go around the parallelogram either way and land in the same spot: a + b = b + a.

The pictureWalk \(a\), then walk \(b\). Stretching an arrow by 2 doubles each step.
The mathAdd and scale coordinate by coordinate: \(a + b = (a_1 + b_1,\ a_2 + b_2)\), \(\ c\,a = (c\,a_1,\ c\,a_2)\).

One more operation, the most important one in this whole course: the dot product. Multiply matching coordinates and add them up: \(a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2\). It's one number, and it measures how much two arrows point the same way: big and positive when they agree, zero when they're at right angles, negative when they oppose. The readout above shows it live; drag \(b\) around \(a\) and watch the sign flip as it crosses the right angle.

\[ a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 = \lVert a \rVert\,\lVert b \rVert \cos\theta \]

Remember this one. A neuron (chapter 3) is a dot product of inputs and weights. Attention (chapter 7) is dot products between words. A matrix, next section, is a stack of dot products.

\(a = (1, 2)\) and \(b = (3, -1)\). What is \(a \cdot b\)?

\(1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot (-1) = 3 - 2 = 1\). Small and positive: the arrows are nearly at right angles, leaning slightly the same way.

Two arrows have a dot product of exactly 0. What do you know about them?

  • They have the same length
  • They point in opposite directions
  • They're at a right angle (or one of them is zero)
\(\cos 90^\circ = 0\), so \(\lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta = 0\). Perpendicular arrows share nothing: walking along one makes no progress along the other.

3. A matrix moves the whole plane

Here's the big idea. Two special arrows, î = (1, 0) and ĵ = (0, 1), are the plane's "steps". Any vector is "x of the first step plus y of the second". Now drag the tips of î and ĵ. The grid follows, and so does every vector on it, because v is still x of the new î plus y of the new ĵ.

Predict before you drag: the Rotate 90° button sends î to (0, 1) and ĵ to (−1, 0). Where does v = (2, 1) land?

  • (1, 2)
  • (−1, 2)
  • (−2, −1)
  • (1, −2)
\(v\) is "2 of î plus 1 of ĵ", and that recipe survives the move: \(2\cdot(0, 1) + 1\cdot(-1, 0) = (-1, 2)\). Press the button and check.

That's all a 2×2 matrix is: the columns say where î and ĵ land. Multiplying a vector by the matrix means "find where it lands". The shaded square started with area 1; its new area is the determinant. It goes negative when space gets flipped over, and zero when the plane is squashed onto a line. Then information is lost: many vectors land on the same spot, and you can't undo the move.

The math, step by step

Write where î and ĵ land as the two columns of a matrix. Then "where does \(v = (x, y)\) land?" is

\[ \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = x \begin{pmatrix} a \\ c \end{pmatrix} + y \begin{pmatrix} b \\ d \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a x + b y \\ c x + d y \end{pmatrix} \]

Two ways to read the same arithmetic. By columns (high level): \(x\) steps of the new î plus \(y\) steps of the new ĵ. By rows (how a computer does it): each output number is a dot product of one row with \(v\): \(ax + by\) is \((a, b) \cdot (x, y)\). A matrix is a stack of dot products.

The determinant is the area of the square after the move, with a sign for flips: \(\det = ad - bc\). For the shear \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\): \(1\cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 0 = 1\), the square slants but keeps its area. For "squash flat" \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\): \(1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0.5 = 0\), the columns point the same way and the plane collapses onto a line.

Doing one move after another is matrix multiplication: \(B(Av) = (BA)v\), where each column of \(BA\) is \(B\) applied to a column of \(A\). Order matters: rotate-then-shear is a different move from shear-then-rotate.

What is the determinant of \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\)?

\(2 \cdot 0.5 - 1 \cdot 1 = 0\). The second column (1, 0.5) is half the first (2, 1), so both basis arrows land on the same line: the plane is squashed flat and the move can't be undone.

4. A neural network layer: a move, then a bend

If you stack matrices you only get another matrix: still straight lines, still a single move. Networks get their power from one tiny extra step after each matrix: a bend. The most common one, ReLU, just says "negative numbers become zero". Watch what it does to the moved grid: everything left of the vertical axis gets folded onto it, everything below the horizontal axis onto that.

Drag î and ĵ again: the layer is "move, then fold". Stack dozens of these, each with its own matrix, and the folds can carve space into almost any shape, like one region for "cat" and another for "dog". Training a network is searching for the matrices that put the folds in the right places. That's where we're headed: next chapter, gradients, how a network finds which way to nudge every number.

The math, step by step

A real layer also adds a bias vector \(b\) (it shifts the plane before the fold, which the picture above leaves out), and its matrix needn't be square. A layer from \(n\) numbers to \(m\) numbers is

\[ h = \operatorname{ReLU}(W x + b), \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n},\ b \in \mathbb{R}^{m},\ \operatorname{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z) \text{ for each number} \]

It has \(m \cdot n + m\) adjustable numbers, the parameters. A digit reader that takes 784 pixels to 128 hidden numbers to 10 scores has \(784 \cdot 128 + 128 + 128 \cdot 10 + 10 = 101{,}770\) of them.

Why the bend is essential: without it, two layers collapse into one. \(W_2 (W_1 x) = (W_2 W_1) x\), a single matrix, however many you stack. With ReLU in between, that algebra no longer works, and each layer can fold space somewhere new.

You stack 50 layers, each just a matrix with no bend in between. What can the stack do that one layer can't?

  • Nothing: it's still one matrix
  • Learn curvier shapes, because it has more numbers
  • Fold the plane 50 times
\(W_{50} \cdots W_2 W_1 = W\), one matrix. That's why every layer in a real network ends with a bend like ReLU.

How many parameters does one layer from 784 inputs to 128 outputs have (weights and biases)?

\(784 \times 128\) weights, one per input-output pair, plus 128 biases: \(100{,}352 + 128 = 100{,}480\).
