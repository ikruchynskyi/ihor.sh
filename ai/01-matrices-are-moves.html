AI from scratch · Chapter 1

Matrices are moves

Every neural network, from a digit recognizer to the model behind a chatbot, is mostly one operation repeated billions of times: multiply by a matrix. So before any "AI", let's get a feel for what a matrix does. Not as a grid of numbers, but as a way of moving space. Drag everything.

1. A vector is an arrow

A vector is a pair of numbers, (x, y), but it's easier to think of it as an arrow from the origin: go x steps right, y steps up. Drag the tip of the blue arrow.

In machine learning, everything becomes a vector: a pixel image is a long list of brightness values, a word becomes a list of a few hundred numbers. Same idea, just with more than two directions to walk in.

2. Adding is walking one arrow, then the other

To add two vectors, walk along the first, then from its tip along the second. Where you end up is the sum. Drag both tips.

Notice you can go around the parallelogram either way and land in the same spot: a + b = b + a.

3. A matrix moves the whole plane

Here's the big idea. Two special arrows, î = (1, 0) and ĵ = (0, 1), are the plane's "steps". Any vector is "x of the first step plus y of the second". Now drag the tips of î and ĵ. The grid follows, and so does every vector on it, because v is still x of the new î plus y of the new ĵ.

That's all a 2×2 matrix is: the columns say where î and ĵ land. Multiplying a vector by the matrix means "find where it lands". The shaded square started with area 1; its new area is the determinant. It goes negative when space gets flipped over, and zero when the plane is squashed onto a line. Then information is lost: many vectors land on the same spot, and you can't undo the move.

4. A neural network layer: a move, then a bend

If you stack matrices you only get another matrix: still straight lines, still a single move. Networks get their power from one tiny extra step after each matrix: a bend. The most common one, ReLU, just says "negative numbers become zero". Watch what it does to the moved grid: everything left of the vertical axis gets folded onto it, everything below the horizontal axis onto that.

Drag î and ĵ again: the layer is "move, then fold". Stack dozens of these, each with its own matrix, and the folds can carve space into almost any shape, like one region for "cat" and another for "dog". Training a network is searching for the matrices that put the folds in the right places. That's where we're headed: next chapter, gradients, how a network finds which way to nudge every number.
